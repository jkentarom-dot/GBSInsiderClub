Career Path — Project SME

Project SME
Shine on the Project

You just got pulled onto a project — a transition, a migration, a CI initiative, a system go-live — on top of your day job. This path shows you how to use that opportunity to get noticed and get promoted.

In a GBS organisation, projects are where scale and leverage are created — and where careers accelerate. When a new entity is integrated, an acquired company is rolled in, or SAP is deployed to a business not yet on the platform, the organisation needs SMEs from the existing teams to support, document, test, and train. That SME is often a young associate. Done well, this is the fastest visibility you will ever get. This six-week path shows you how to do it well.

The Path at a Glance

Before You Start

How this path works

Six weeks, alongside the project

This path runs in parallel with your real project assignment. Each week maps to a phase you will actually move through — from getting pulled in, to go-live, to converting the experience into a career step.

Your expertise is your leverage

On a project, your process knowledge is the scarcest resource in the room. The project cannot succeed without it. This path teaches you to use that leverage without burning out or overstepping.

Templates you actually use

KT checklist, UAT test script, RAID log entry, go-live readiness check, and an SME-to-career positioning sheet. Built to be edited, ready for your real project.

The Opportunity

Why the SME role is a hidden career accelerator

Most associates see a project assignment as extra work on top of BAU. The ones who advance see it for what it is: an audition in front of people who do not normally see them.

New eyes see you

Project managers, workstream leads, and senior stakeholders from across the organisation are suddenly watching how you work. These are people who would never see your BAU performance — and some of them make hiring decisions.

New entities, new roles

When a business is integrated or migrated, new roles open for the new scope. SMEs who supported the transition are the natural candidates — you already know the processes and the people. Transitions create org charts; be on them.

From SME to trainer to lead

New joiners are hired for the new entity. Someone has to train them. That someone is often the SME who documented the process. Training others is a leadership signal — and a direct step toward a Senior Associate or Team Lead role.

Julian's Take

In every transition I ran, the associates who treated it as "extra work" stayed associates. The ones who treated it as a stage — who documented cleanly, tested rigorously, trained the new joiners, and made themselves visible to the project leadership — got pulled into the new structure. The work is the same. The framing is everything.

1
Project SME — Week 1

Understand the Project and Your Place in It

Before you do anything, understand what kind of project you are on, what is expected of you, and how to protect your BAU. Confusion in week one costs you the whole project.

1.1Project vs. BAU — Learn the Different Rules▾

Project work runs on different rules than your day job. BAU rewards consistency and throughput. Projects reward hitting milestones, flagging risks early, and clear communication across people who do not share your context. The first mistake associates make is running their project work like BAU — heads down, just executing — and missing that on a project, visible communication is part of the deliverable.

The project types you will meet in GBS
Project typeWhat you'll do as SMEWhy it matters
Transition / migration (roll-in of a new entity)Document current state, support knowledge transfer, test, train new joinersThis is where GBS scale is built — highest visibility, most new roles
System deployment (SAP / ERP go-live)Validate process design, write and run test cases, support hypercareOften combined with a transition; deep exposure to process and technology
CI / Lean Six Sigma projectProvide process reality, collect data, validate improvementsYour ground truth keeps the project honest; belt-holders need you
Controls / compliance initiativeMap controls, document gaps, support remediationBuilds a compliance skill set that opens audit and risk career tracks
Julian's Take

If you are on a transition that combines an SAP deployment with a company roll-in, you are in the best possible spot. That is the GBS growth engine in motion. The new entity needs people who understand both the old way and the new platform — and after go-live, those people are exactly who gets offered the new roles. Treat it accordingly.

AI Application

Brief your AI with your Session Document, then ask: "I've been assigned as SME to a [transition / SAP go-live / CI] project while keeping my BAU. Interview me about my role, then produce a one-page summary of what is expected of me, what good looks like, and the three biggest risks to my BAU."

1.2Protect Your BAU — The Capacity Conversation▾

The fastest way to fail at both is to accept the project on top of a full BAU load with no protected time. You will burn out, your BAU quality will slip, and the project will suffer — and somehow it will look like your fault. Have the capacity conversation with your manager before the project ramps, not after you are drowning.

What to agree before you start
Agree thisWhy
Protected project time (e.g. X days/week or % allocation)Without it, project work happens on top of everything — unsustainable
What BAU gets covered or pausedSomeone must absorb your BAU, or it silently fails
Who your project point of contact isYou need one clear channel for project asks, not five
How conflicts get escalatedWhen BAU and project collide, you need an agreed rule, not a panic
Practical Exercise

Use the SME Participation Agreement template to draft your capacity ask. Walk your manager through it this week. The goal is one page, agreed, that protects you when the project gets intense.

Session Recap — Why it matters

Knowing the rules of project work — versus BAU — is what stops you failing at both.

  • I understand which project type I'm on and its rules
  • I have agreed protected time and BAU coverage with my manager
  • I know my single project point of contact
2
Project SME — Week 2

Knowledge Transfer — Document Like a Professional

Knowledge transfer is where SMEs are made or broken. The quality of your documentation now determines how much pain everyone feels at go-live — and how good you look doing it.

2.1The 4-Phase KT Model — Done Properly▾

Knowledge transfer is not "show someone your screen once." Real KT moves through four phases, and skipping any of them is why processes break after go-live. As the SME, you own the source-of-truth knowledge. How you transfer it is your reputation.

The four phases
PhaseWhat happensYour SME job
1. DocumentCapture the process as it really runsWrite SOPs / DTPs that include the exceptions, not just the happy path
2. DemonstrateShow the process liveWalk through real cases, including the messy ones
3. Practise (with support)The receiver does it, you watchLet them drive; correct gently; resist taking the keyboard
4. Validate (independent)The receiver does it alone, measuredSign off only when they genuinely can — your name is on it
Julian's Take

The exceptions are the whole game. Anyone can document the happy path. The value of a real SME is knowing what happens when the invoice has no PO, when the entity uses a different tax code, when the month-end falls on a holiday. Document the exceptions and you are irreplaceable. Skip them and the new team drowns in week one — and everyone remembers whose KT it was.

AI Application

Describe a process to your AI and ask: "Turn this into a structured SOP with a clear happy path, then a separate exceptions section. Interview me with 10 questions to surface the edge cases I forgot to mention." The interview is where the real knowledge comes out.

Quick check

You've documented the happy path perfectly and signed off the KT. Why might go-live still be painful?

2.2Become the Trainer — Turn KT into a Leadership Signal▾

When a new entity is integrated, new joiners are hired for the new scope — and they need training. The SME who documented the process is the obvious trainer. Volunteering for this is one of the highest-leverage moves an associate can make: training others is the clearest pre-promotion leadership signal there is.

Why training is a career move, not extra work
  • Visibility to leadership — you are now the person the project trusts with new people.
  • A leadership rehearsal — explaining, correcting, and building others' capability is exactly what a Team Lead does.
  • A network of people who owe you — every person you train remembers who got them started.
Practical Exercise

Offer to build the onboarding checklist for one process the new joiners will need. Use your KT documentation as the base. Present it to your project lead — this single act moves you from "SME" to "trainer" in their mind.

Session Recap — Why it matters

The exceptions are the whole game. Skip them and the new team drowns at go-live.

  • My SOPs include a real exceptions section, not just the happy path
  • I've moved at least one process through all four KT phases
  • I've volunteered to train or build onboarding for new joiners
3
Project SME — Week 3

Testing — UAT Without the Guesswork

On a system deployment, you will be asked to test. Most associates do it badly because nobody taught them how. Good testing makes go-live smooth and makes you look sharp; bad testing causes a painful go-live with your name on the sign-off.

3.1UAT That Actually Catches Problems▾

User Acceptance Testing is your chance to confirm the new system or process actually works for the real business before it goes live. The goal is not to click through and confirm it works — it is to deliberately try to break it the way reality will. A test that only checks the happy path guarantees a painful go-live.

How to test properly
Do thisNot this
Write test cases from real scenarios, including exceptionsImprovise clicks and "see if it works"
Use real-world data variety (different entities, currencies, tax codes)Test one clean example and sign off
Document each result clearly: pass / fail / what happenedRemember it in your head
Re-test after fixes (regression)Assume a fix didn't break something else
Julian's Take

When you sign off on UAT, you are telling the organisation "this works for the business I represent." If you waved it through and it breaks at go-live, that is on you — and people remember. If you caught the issue everyone else missed because you knew the one exception that matters, that is also remembered. Testing is a reputation event. Treat it like one.

AI Application

Give your AI a process and ask: "Generate a UAT test script covering the happy path plus 8 edge cases for [process]. For each, give the steps, the test data, and the expected result." Then validate and improve it with your real knowledge — the AI gives you structure, you supply the exceptions that matter.

Project SME — Week 4

Communicate on a Project — RAID and Status

On a project, the people deciding your reputation often only see you through your written updates. Learn to raise issues without looking negative, and to report status so leadership trusts you.

4.1RAID Logs — Raise Issues Without Looking Negative▾

RAID stands for Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies — the project's shared memory of what could go wrong and what it is counting on. As an SME, you will spot risks others miss because you know the process. Raising them well marks you as switched-on; raising them badly marks you as a complainer. The difference is entirely in the framing.

The four RAID elements
ElementWhat it capturesSME example
RiskSomething that might go wrong"If the legacy tax codes aren't mapped, payments will fail at go-live"
AssumptionSomething being taken as true"We assume the new entity uses the same approval hierarchy"
IssueA problem happening now"Test data for entity X is not available"
DependencySomething you need from someone else"KT sign-off depends on the entity finance lead's availability"

The framing rule: raise a risk with a proposed mitigation, never as a bare complaint. "X is broken" is negativity. "X is a risk; here's what I'd suggest we do" is leadership.

Practical Exercise

Use the RAID Log template to write your first three entries from your real project — at least one risk with a proposed mitigation. Submit it to your project lead this week.

Session Recap — Why it matters

A clean go-live is built in testing. Your sign-off is a reputation event.

  • I've written UAT test cases that include exceptions
  • I've logged at least one risk with a proposed mitigation
  • I've submitted one clear project status update

Project SME — Week 5

Go-Live and Hypercare — Land It Cleanly

Go-live is the moment everything is judged. Hypercare is the support period right after. How you show up here is what people remember when the project is over and roles are being filled.

5.1Go-Live Readiness and Hypercare Support▾

Go-live readiness means confirming, honestly, that your area is ready: processes documented, people trained, tests passed, exceptions known. Hypercare is the intensive support window right after go-live when issues surface and need fast resolution. SMEs who are calm, available, and organised during hypercare become the people the new organisation wants to keep.

Your go-live readiness check
  • Documentation current — SOPs reflect the new system, exceptions included.
  • People ready — new joiners trained and validated, not just shown.
  • Issues logged — known problems are in the RAID log, not just in your head.
  • Support plan clear — you know who to escalate to and how fast.
Julian's Take

Hypercare is the audition's final act. Everyone is stressed, things are breaking, and the person who stays calm, tracks every issue, and quietly resolves things is the one leadership notices. That is the person who gets the offer for the new entity's permanent role. Be that person — organised, unflappable, visible.

Practical Exercise

Build your area's go-live readiness assessment using the template. Walk it through with your project lead and be honest about gaps — flagging a gap before go-live is professional; hiding it is a career risk.

Project SME — Week 6

Convert the Project into a Career Step

The project is ending. Most people just go back to BAU. You are going to convert what you did into evidence, visibility, and a concrete next move — because that was the point all along.

6.1From SME to Your Next Role▾

You documented processes, trained new joiners, caught issues at UAT, and supported a clean go-live. That is a portfolio. Now make it visible and use it. Transitions create new roles; the SMEs who supported them are the natural candidates — but only if leadership connects you to the work.

Your conversion checklist
ActionWhy
Log every project contribution in your Brag Sheet with numbers"Trained 6 new joiners; my area had zero critical go-live defects" is promotion evidence
Ask your project lead for written feedbackA sentence from a project leader carries weight in calibration
Signal interest in roles for the new entityIf you don't say you want it, the pull reason can't work for you
Update your CV and LinkedIn with the projectTransition and migration experience is genuinely marketable
Julian's Take

Here is the pattern I most want you to avoid. An associate does brilliant project work, then says nothing and slides back into BAU. The project leadership moves on, the new roles get filled by people who made their interest known, and six months later that associate wonders why they were overlooked. So do the work — then make sure the right people know you did it, and that you want what comes next.

Practical Exercise

Use the SME-to-Career Positioning sheet: list your project contributions with numbers, draft your interest message to your project lead or manager, and identify the specific next role the transition might open. Send the message this week.

Session Recap — Why it matters

Brilliant project work means nothing to your career if no one connects it to you.

  • Every project contribution is logged in my Brag Sheet with numbers
  • I've asked my project lead for written feedback
  • I've signalled interest in a specific next role to the right person
