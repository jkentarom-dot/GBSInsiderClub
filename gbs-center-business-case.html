4. Program plan: Waves and timing

The view starts in month 1 and shows the whole program. Month 1 is the first hire, or the start of set-up if you add set-up months before the first hire. Hiring in the new center builds up wave by wave while the jobs in the countries go down. A wave is a group of workstreams that is hired and moved together, for example payables first, then receivables, then record to report. Waves can overlap and run in parallel. Each wave is hired over its months to move; its teams go live after the double-cost months (3.C.1), in the same pace. Savings (1), operating costs (2) and one-time costs (3) follow this calendar. The plan is the same for all three options.

Money in later years counts less. Most companies use 7 to 10.

7. Risks and data quality

How the calculator works

Method, what is included and what is not
  1. Cost today. For each country and role: Jobs times average pay, plus bonus, employer charges and office. IT is left out: It moves with the job and costs the same before and after. It grows each year with wages in those countries. Jobs leave wave by wave, in equal monthly steps inside each wave: They move to the new center, end, or are replaced by automation. Month 1 is the first hire, or the start of set-up if you add set-up months before it. Offices that are kept stay in the cost.
  2. Run cost of the new center. Jobs that do the work times the cost per job (pay, bonus, employer charges, language bonus and office), plus the fixed site running cost, plus the turnover difference to today, plus the running cost of automation. After the move you need fewer people each year (efficiency). Cost grows with wages at the site: A site that is 5 percent cheaper today but grows 2 to 3 points faster per year is more expensive by year 4.
  3. Two sites in one option. You split the jobs by share over two sites. Cost per job, rent and data confidence are blended by share of jobs. Wage growth and turnover are blended by cost. Each site pays its own running cost and set-up. Two things are added: A cross-site coordination cost each year, and a larger project team. Both are estimates; Replace them with your own numbers. The blend uses one wage growth rate for the whole center, a small simplification: The two sites do not drift apart over the view.
  4. One-time cost, in three blocks. A, people leaving: Severance, outplacement and retention bonuses are paid when each job leaves; Works council and legal advice in the months before the first team is live. B, site: Site set-up and office fit-out in the same months, IT equipment when people are hired. A fit-out lasts about 10 years: Unused value at the end of a view comes back as money, and a worn-out fit-out is paid again. C, transition: New people paid before they take over the work (double cost), extra people for the first 12 months after each team goes live, recruiting and travel when people are hired, the project team from month 1 to the last go-live, and the automation build in the months before the first team is live. D, incentives and income: Grants and office sale come in when the move is done, tax relief each month after go-live. In time, the one-time cost falls in five phases, shown in section 6: Set-up (month 1 until the first team is live), hiring and training (from each hire), people leaving (at each go-live), project team, and grants and income. Each wave is hired over its months to move, and each person is trained for the double-cost months before the old job ends.
  5. Net effect. Cost today minus what is still paid at the old locations, minus run cost of the new center, plus other savings, minus one-time cost. Month by month from month 1 to the end of the chosen view (5 years by default). Set-up and the move period are inside the view. Costs before month 1, for example the decision phase, are not counted.
  6. Other savings. Temps and overtime, licenses, audit fees and outside services, as a share of the labor cost of the moved jobs. Each item can be changed or switched off, and you can add one item of your own. By default they are shown next to the result and are not part of the net effect or the payback. You can choose to count them in.
  7. Results. Payback is the first month the running total is above zero. NPV is the total from month 1 to the end of the view, with later money counted less. Longer views carry more risk: Wages, volumes and the efficiency gain are forecasts. The efficiency gain stops after the years set in 2.1.g. Read it as a direction, not as a promise. IRR is the yearly return on the money you put in. This is a cash view: Severance is booked as a provision when the move is announced, and fit-out is capex written off over its life. Your P&L shows these at other times than the cash does.

Not included: tax and incentives, transfer pricing between your companies, exchange rates, more than two sites in one option, different go-live dates for the two sites, and a different wave plan for each option. These usually move the result by 10 to 20 percent.

Data confidence. Every country and site has a confidence score: Green 70 percent and above (recent salary guides), amber 50 to 69 percent (partly estimated), red below 50 percent (mostly estimates). The result cards show the score for the site and for your countries. With two sites the score is blended by share of jobs. Where the score is low, replace the averages with your own numbers in the detail settings.

How exact is it? We checked it against a detailed month-by-month model with the same cost levels. The difference was about 4 percent of the 5-year cost, and payback differed by 0 to 5 months. That check used the earlier 60-month view with one wave. The wave plan and the quarter table came later and were checked against the one-wave results: With one wave they give the same cash flow. Your real cost per job matters much more than that difference. Enter your roles per country, or your own payroll cost per FTE.
