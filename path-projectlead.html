Career Booster — Step 1

Project Leadership Mindset

Leading a project is a fundamentally different job than contributing to one. Step 1 sets the mindset: you are now responsible for an outcome you cannot deliver alone, through people who do not report to you.

1.1From Contributor to Leader — The Real Shift▾
Key principle

Your job is now the team result, achieved through others.

As a contributor, you were measured on your own work. As a project lead, you are measured on whether the project delivers — which depends almost entirely on other people, most of whom do not report to you. This is leading without authority, and it is the core skill. The people who struggle as project leads are the ones who try to do all the work themselves; the ones who succeed orchestrate, unblock, and hold the outcome.

The mindset shifts that matter
From (contributor)To (project lead)
Doing the workMaking sure the work happens, by others
My task is doneThe outcome is delivered, end to end
Authority comes from my titleInfluence comes from clarity, trust, and momentum
Problems are escalated to meI anticipate problems and clear them before they hit
Julian's Take

The hardest lesson for first-time project leads is that doing the work yourself feels productive but is actually you failing at your real job. If you are heads-down executing tasks, nobody is leading the project. Your job is to see the whole board, keep everyone moving, and remove the obstacles only you can remove. That feels less busy and is far more valuable.

AI Application

Brief your AI with your project context and ask: "I'm leading [project]. Interview me about scope, stakeholders, and timeline, then tell me honestly where I'm likely to fall into 'doing the work myself' instead of leading, and how to avoid it."

Julian's Take

You will feel the temptation to push harder the moment a project wobbles. Resist the reflex. First read the temperature of the team — morale dips have causes: workload, fatigue, shifting priorities, or plain pressure. Name the real one before you act. If the pressure is structural, take it to the sponsor or steerco and ask them to adjust the plan; that is their job. And if the honest cause is your own communication or how you are running things, look in the mirror, talk to the team, and adapt — get coaching, borrow help from people who have done it. Pride is a poor advisor when a project is under strain.

1.2Scope the Project Honestly — Where 80% Fail▾
Remember

Most projects fail at scoping. Get it tight before you start.

Most projects fail at scoping, not execution. Too big, too vague, or no clear definition of done — and the project drifts until everyone is exhausted and nothing shipped. A tight scope is the single biggest predictor of delivery. Before anything else, you define what this project is, what it is not, and how you'll know it's done.

The scoping questions you must answer
QuestionWhy it matters
What specific problem does this solve?A project without a sharp problem statement cannot succeed — there's no target
What is explicitly out of scope?Naming what you won't do prevents scope creep later
What does "done" look like, measurably?Without a definition of done, the project never ends
Who is the sponsor and what do they want?The sponsor's real goal is the project's true north
Practical Exercise

Use the Project Charter template to scope your real project on one page: problem, objectives, in/out of scope, success criteria, sponsor. Get it reviewed and signed off before you do anything else. An approved charter is your shield against scope creep.

Session Recap — Why it matters

If you're heads-down executing, nobody is leading the project.

  • I understand my job is to lead the outcome, not do all the work
  • My project has a sharp, written problem statement
  • I have a signed-off Project Charter with a clear definition of done

Quick check

Your project keeps growing as stakeholders add 'just one more thing.' What should have prevented this?

A signed-off Project Charter with an explicit Out of Scope section. Most projects fail at scoping, not execution. When someone asks for more, you point at what was agreed — and any real change becomes a charter decision with the sponsor, not a quiet expansion that sinks your timeline.

Career Booster — Step 2

Master the Craft — Plan and Run the Project

This is the toolkit. Planning, structured problem-solving, governance, and the methods that make delivery predictable instead of heroic. Use the depth your project needs.

2.1Break It Down — WBS and the Plan▾
The real game

Break the work down until each piece is ownable and estimable.

A project is delivered by breaking a large outcome into small, ownable pieces of work, each with an owner and a date. The Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) is how you do this. A real plan is the shared agreement on who does what by when — and your basis for tracking whether you're on track.

From outcome to plan
LevelWhat it isExample (tool rollout)
OutcomeThe project's goalNew ticketing tool live for 3 teams
WorkstreamA major area of workConfiguration · Data migration · Training · Go-live
Work packageAn ownable chunk"Migrate historical tickets" — owner, dates
TaskA single action"Export legacy data to staging"

Every work package needs an owner and a date. A plan where everything is owned by "the team" is owned by no one.

Practical Exercise

Use the WBS template to break your project into workstreams and work packages. Assign an owner and a date to each. This becomes your master plan and your tracking baseline.

2.2Choose Your Method — CI, Waterfall, or Agile▾
Field truth

Match the method to the project. The wrong one fights you the whole way.

Match the method to the project

Different projects need different methods. Picking the wrong one creates friction the whole way through. You don't need to be a certified expert in all of them — you need to know which fits your project and how to run it competently.

Match the method to the project
MethodBest forCore tools
DMAIC / Lean Six SigmaProcess improvement, reducing defects or wasteSIPOC, root cause, control plan
A3 / KaizenFocused improvement events, fast cyclesOne-page A3, 5 Whys, fishbone
WaterfallDefined-scope deployments with clear stagesWBS, gate reviews, milestones
Agile / iterativeEvolving requirements, software-style deliveryBacklog, sprints, stand-ups
Julian's Take

In GBS, most associate-led projects are CI or deployment work, so DMAIC and a clean Waterfall with gate reviews cover most of what you'll run. Don't over-engineer the method. A simple, well-run A3 beats a badly-run pseudo-Agile process every time. Pick the lightest method that fits, and run it properly.

AI Application

Describe your project to your AI and ask: "Given this scope, timeline, and certainty of requirements, which delivery method fits best — DMAIC, A3, Waterfall, or Agile — and why? Give me the decision in a short table."

2.3Structured Problem-Solving — A3 and Root Cause▾
Do this

Solve the root cause, not the symptom. Structure beats "we fixed it".

Project leaders are paid to solve problems in a way others can follow and trust. "We fixed it" is weak. A structured approach — define the problem, find the real root cause, design the solution, prove it worked — is what impresses sponsors and what holds up when someone challenges your results.

The structured path
  • Define — state the problem with data, not opinion.
  • Analyse — find the root cause (5 Whys, fishbone), not the symptom.
  • Solve — design options, choose with the team, plan the change.
  • Control — prove the fix held with a measure that doesn't regress.
Practical Exercise

Take the central problem in your project and run it through the A3 template: problem, current state, root cause, target state, countermeasures, plan. One page. Present it to your sponsor — a clean A3 builds more credibility than a long deck.

Session Recap — Why it matters

Work owned by 'the team' is owned by no one. Owners and dates make a plan real.

  • My project is broken into a WBS with owners and dates
  • I've chosen a delivery method that fits the project
  • I've run my core problem through a structured A3
2.4Govern the Project — RAID and Gate Reviews▾
First move

Governance prevents most derailment. Gate reviews are your safety net.

Governance sounds bureaucratic but it is what prevents projects from quietly derailing. Two tools do most of the work: a live RAID log (risks, assumptions, issues, dependencies) and gate reviews (formal go/no-go checkpoints between phases). Together they mean problems surface early and the project can't drift past a broken stage.

Lightweight governance that works
ToolWhat it doesCadence
RAID logTracks what could go wrong and what you're counting onReviewed weekly, owned by you
Gate reviewFormal checkpoint: are we ready for the next phase?At each phase boundary
Status reportTells stakeholders where things stand, honestlyWeekly or bi-weekly
Decision logRecords what was decided and whyAs decisions happen
Julian's Take

Governance prevents about 90% of project derailment, and most of it is just discipline: a RAID log you actually update, and the courage to call a gate review honestly even when the answer is "we're not ready." The project lead who says "we're not ready, here's the gap, here's the plan" looks far stronger six months later than the one who waved a broken project through the gate to avoid an awkward meeting.

Practical Exercise

Set up your RAID log from the template and populate it with your real project's current risks, assumptions, issues, and dependencies. Commit to a weekly review. This single habit prevents most surprises.

Julian's Take

Two failure modes show up once a project is running, and they need different reflexes. Slipping: run a tight status cadence where teams report delays out loud, agree the support they need — people, scope, a moved deadline — and require a root cause, a mitigation, and a prevention for every miss. That rhythm builds urgency and ownership without you chasing. Scope creep: do not absorb a single addition on goodwill. Anything new goes through the steering committee with its cost in time, budget, and quality on the table. If the scope did grow, come back with a re-baselined plan for re-approval, or a catch-up plan. Silent absorption is how good projects quietly fail.

Career Booster — Step 3

Stakeholders, Visibility, and Communication

Projects live or die on stakeholder management. As project lead you are constantly communicating up, across, and out — and that visibility is also building your reputation. Use it deliberately.

3.1Map and Manage Your Stakeholders▾
Bottom line

Map power and interest first. Manage each group differently.

Every project has people who can help it, block it, or sink it. Knowing who they are, what they care about, and how much power and interest they hold lets you manage them deliberately instead of being surprised. The stakeholder who feels ignored becomes the one who undermines you in a steering meeting.

The power / interest grid
QuadrantWhoHow to manage
High power, high interestSponsor, key decision-makersManage closely — frequent, tailored communication
High power, low interestSenior leaders not close to the projectKeep satisfied — concise updates, no surprises
Low power, high interestAffected teams, end usersKeep informed — they shape adoption
Low power, low interestPeripheral partiesMonitor — light touch
Practical Exercise

Use the Stakeholder Map template to plot your project's real stakeholders on the power/interest grid. For each high-power stakeholder, write one line: what they care about and how you'll keep them on side.

Julian's Take

A steering committee that will not decide is usually one that has not been given comfort. Bring them options with pros and cons and a clear recommendation, rather than an open question. Clarity, simple messages, and solid prep remove the fear of the wrong call, which is what stalls most approvals. Leaders ask the same things in patterns: what are the risks, what are the options, what is the cost or time impact, and who is already on board. Answer those before they are asked. Pre-align your key stakeholders in private; if you can tell the room they already agree, your decision lands. And when someone pushes new scope in, show the impact on time, quality, and cost — so at minimum the sponsor owns the consequence with eyes open.

3.2Manage Your Sponsor — They Don't Manage Themselves▾
Key principle

Sponsors do not manage themselves. Drive that relationship deliberately.

Your sponsor is the most important stakeholder — they hold the budget, the authority, and the air cover. But sponsors are busy and will not manage the relationship for you. A disengaged sponsor is the single biggest predictor of project failure. Managing up to your sponsor is your job, not theirs.

How to keep a sponsor engaged
  • Make it easy — bring them clear decisions to make, not open problems to solve.
  • No surprises — a sponsor who hears bad news first from someone else stops trusting you.
  • Use their power deliberately — when you're blocked at your level, that's exactly what a sponsor is for.
Julian's Take

Treat your sponsor like a resource you have to actively deploy, not a figurehead who shows up at the end. Bring them the two or three decisions only they can make, give them the context in one page, and tell them exactly what you need. Sponsors love a project lead who makes their involvement efficient — and they remember them when bigger roles open up.

AI Application

Ask your AI: "Help me prepare a sponsor update. Here's my status: [paste]. Structure it as: 3-line summary, decisions I need from you, key risks, and what I'm handling myself. Keep it to one screen."

Julian's Take

You are leading people who do not report to you, and a sponsor who can outrank everyone in the room. Both extend trust based on what they have seen you deliver. It is built in observable moments: you said the number would be ready Friday and it was; you flagged the risk before it landed; you carried the bad news yourself instead of letting them find it. Run the sponsor relationship on no surprises. The fastest way to lose that trust is a broken promise, or partial information when something is going wrong — and there is no shortcut back, only honesty and consistency repeated over time.

Session Recap — Why it matters

The stakeholder who feels ignored becomes the one who undermines you in the steering meeting.

  • I've mapped my stakeholders on the power/interest grid
  • I have a deliberate plan for each high-power stakeholder
  • I'm managing my sponsor with clear decisions and no surprises

Career Booster — Step 4

Land It and Prove the Value

Delivery means a clean landing, adoption that sticks, and value proven in terms Finance accepts. This is what separates a project that happened from a project that mattered.

4.1Change Management — Make the Change Stick▾
Remember

A solution nobody adopts saved nothing. Make the change stick.

Make the change stick

A technically perfect project that nobody adopts is a failure. People resist change — not because they're difficult, but because change is disruptive and uncertain. Managing the human side is as important as managing the plan. The best solution implemented badly loses to a decent solution people actually embrace.

The change essentials
Do thisWhy
Explain the "why" before the "what"People accept change they understand the reason for
Involve the affected people earlyInvolvement converts resistance into ownership
Communicate more than feels necessaryIn change, under-communication is the default failure
Support through the dipPerformance drops before it improves — plan for it
Julian's Take

Resistance is usually a signal, not an obstacle. When people push back, they often see a real problem you missed from the project room. Listen first. The associate who treats resistance as enemy fire escalates conflict; the one who treats it as feedback wins the room — and delivers a change that actually holds after go-live.

4.2Prove the Value — Hard vs. Soft Savings▾
The real game

Only validated hard savings count on the P&L. Get Finance to sign off.

When your project ends, leadership and Finance will ask what it delivered. How you answer determines whether your project is remembered as a success. The critical distinction: hard savings reduce actual spend (FTEs, licenses, vendor cost) and land on the P&L; soft savings (freed time, better quality, reduced risk) are real but don't directly cut the budget. Claiming soft as hard destroys your credibility with Finance — permanently.

Get your value validated
Saving typeExampleCounts on P&L?
HardRemoved 1.5 FTE of manual effort, redeployed; cancelled a licenseYes — if the reduction is real and actioned
SoftFreed 5 hours/week of capacity; fewer errors; lower riskNo — valuable, but not direct P&L money
Practical Exercise

Use the Status Report template's benefits section to document your project's value, clearly split into hard and soft. Take it to Finance and get the hard number validated. A Finance-validated saving is the most powerful line in your career story.

Session Recap — Why it matters

A technically perfect project nobody adopts is a failure. The human side is the project.

  • I have a change plan for the people affected, not just the process
  • My project's value is split honestly into hard and soft savings
  • I've had the hard savings validated by Finance

Quick check

Your project freed up 5 hours a week across the team. Can you report this as a hard saving to Finance?

No — that's a soft saving until it converts into an actual reduction in spend (FTEs, licenses, vendor cost). Freed time is real and valuable, but it only becomes a hard, P&L-counting saving when capacity is genuinely removed or redeployed. Claiming soft as hard is the fastest way to lose Finance's trust permanently.

Career Booster — Step 5

Execute Your Career Move

A delivered project is career currency — but only if you convert it. Step 5 turns your project portfolio into a concrete next step toward bigger initiatives, a PM role, or management.

5.1Build Your Project Portfolio and Make the Move▾
Field truth

Your project portfolio is career currency for Manager and Director roles.

Project leadership is the most portable, most marketable evidence in a GBS career. A delivered initiative with a validated number is worth more in a promotion case than years of solid BAU. Now you make it visible and use it — for the next, bigger project, for a project management role, or as the bridge into management.

Convert delivery into advancement
ActionWhy
Document each project: scope, your role, result, validated valueThis is your portfolio — the core of every future promotion case
Get sponsor and stakeholder feedback in writingSenior endorsement carries disproportionate weight in calibration
Signal your appetite for bigger scopeThe pull reason only works if leadership knows you want more
Position the experience on LinkedIn and your CVTransformation and project delivery are highly marketable externally
Julian's Take

The project leads who advance fastest treat every initiative as a portfolio piece. They finish, they quantify, they get the sponsor's endorsement on record, and they make their interest in the next, bigger thing known — before the project is even fully closed. Delivery earns you the right to ask. Asking is still on you.

Practical Exercise

Build your project portfolio entry for the initiative you're running: scope, your role, the validated result, and one sponsor quote. Then identify the next bigger project or role you want — and tell the person who can make it happen.

Julian's Take

Complex project delivery is the strongest career currency you can build at this level — but be deliberate about where it points. For some it is the route into a Manager or Director seat; for others it is the foundation of a transformation or programme career. Both are real. The trap is letting pride, rather than evidence, choose the next step. Look at what you actually delivered and what it qualifies you for. One field caution: brand-new, loosely defined roles can look like a promotion and are the first to disappear when budgets tighten. Aim for a seat with a clear mandate and a clear owner.

AI Application — Resume vs. Requirements

Starter. "Here is my CV and a job description for [target role, e.g. GBS Manager or Programme Lead]. List the gaps between what I have and what they want."

Pro. "Act as a GBS hiring manager. Compare my CV against this [target role] job description. Group the gaps into three buckets: must-have I am missing, have-but-cannot-evidence, and nice-to-have. For each must-have gap, propose how a current or upcoming project could close it inside 90 days."

Meta. "Before I paste anything, ask me the eight questions you need to judge my readiness for [target role] against my real project portfolio."

Then validate. AI overstates fit. Cross-check every claimed strength against a real example from the last three months. No example means it is a development goal, not a strength yet.

Session Recap — Why it matters

A delivered project with a validated number outweighs years of solid BAU in a promotion case.

  • My project is documented as a portfolio piece with a validated number
  • I have written sponsor or stakeholder feedback on record
  • I've signalled my appetite for bigger scope to the right person
