FIELD NOTES

Relationships change the moment you get the title. Former colleagues — even friends — will communicate differently, trust you less, watch more closely. That's normal, not personal. Treat everyone the same from day one. Being visibly closer to some people creates a favoritism perception that's almost impossible to undo once it sets in. Where new leaders tend to go wrong first: they default to micromanagement as a way to manage their own anxiety, and they pass pressure down one-to-one — whatever comes from above lands on the team unfiltered. The leaders who survive the first 90 days learn to absorb pressure, not relay it.

7.1 New Manager

The Peer-to-Boss Transition
Team Lead
+

Yesterday they were your peers. Today you are their manager. The relationship shifts overnight but the trust takes months to rebuild on new terms. The biggest mistake new managers make is either trying too hard to stay friends (avoiding difficult conversations) or overcompensating with authority (micromanaging). The balance is professional warmth with clear expectations.

Delegation: The Art of Letting Go
Team Lead
+

Delegation is not dumping tasks — it is transferring ownership with context, authority, and accountability. New managers struggle with this because they could do the work faster themselves. That is exactly the trap. If you cannot delegate, your team capacity is limited to your personal bandwidth, and you never develop the people who need to grow into the work.

The First 90 Days: Building Credibility
Team Lead
+

The first 90 days as a new manager follow three phases: listen (days 1-30), diagnose (days 31-60), and act (days 61-90). Rushing to make changes before you understand the team, the politics, and the history creates resistance. Taking too long to act after you understand the situation signals indecisiveness. The 90-day framework paces the transition.

Self-Awareness and Stress Management (EQ)
Team Lead
+

Emotional intelligence is not soft — it is the hardest skill to develop and the most visible when absent. Self-awareness means knowing your triggers, your blind spots, and how your behavior affects your team. Stress management means maintaining composure when things go wrong. Your team watches how you handle pressure and calibrates their own response accordingly.

7.2 Team Operations

Resource Capacity Planning and Demand Forecasting
Team Lead
+

Capacity planning matches available FTEs to expected work volume — factoring in leave, training, attrition, and seasonal spikes. Demand forecasting predicts incoming volume based on business cycles, month-end patterns, and pipeline data. Without both, you are either overstaffed (expensive) or understaffed (SLA breaches and burnout). The model does not need to be perfect — it needs to be directionally right.

Hiring: Behavioral Interviewing and Selection
Team Lead
+

Behavioral interviewing uses past behavior as a predictor of future performance. "Tell me about a time you handled a conflicting deadline" reveals more than "How would you handle a conflicting deadline?" The structured format — situation, task, action, result — gives you comparable data across candidates and reduces the bias of gut-feel hiring decisions.

DEI in Hiring: Reducing Bias in Global Teams
Team Lead
+

Diverse teams outperform homogeneous ones — the research is clear. The challenge is structural: bias enters through job descriptions, sourcing channels, interview panels, and evaluation criteria. Reducing bias requires intentional design at every stage, not just good intentions. In global GBS teams, diversity also means navigating cultural norms around directness, hierarchy, and self-promotion.

Onboarding and Knowledge Ramp-up
Team Lead
+

The first 60 days of a new hire determine whether they become productive or start looking for their next role. Effective onboarding is not a welcome email and a SharePoint link — it is a structured ramp-up plan with clear milestones, a buddy system, and regular check-ins. The investment in the first two months pays back over the entire tenure.

Succession Planning and Single Points of Failure
Project Mgr
+

A single point of failure (SPOF) is any team member whose departure would cause operational disruption because nobody else can do their job. In GBS, SPOFs are everywhere — the one person who knows the legacy reconciliation, the only analyst who can run the month-end close report. Succession planning eliminates SPOFs through cross-training, documentation, and deliberate knowledge transfer.

Remote Team Culture Building
Team Lead
+

Culture in a remote GBS team does not happen by accident — it is designed. Regular one-on-ones, virtual team rituals, deliberate informal interaction, and visible recognition all contribute. The teams that struggle with remote culture are the ones that replaced office proximity with more meetings instead of building asynchronous trust and connection.

7.3 Performance Management

Coaching vs. Managing: The Distinction
Team Lead
+

Managing tells people what to do. Coaching helps people figure out what to do. Both are necessary — you manage the process and coach the person. The distinction matters because over-managing kills initiative and over-coaching wastes time on problems that need a directive answer. The skill is reading which approach the moment requires.

Read full guide →
The PIP Process: Managing Low Performers
Team Lead
+

A Performance Improvement Plan is not a termination document — it is a structured last chance. Done well, it gives the employee clear expectations, measurable targets, a support plan, and a timeline. Done poorly, it is a paper trail that damages trust and morale. The key is that by the time you issue a PIP, there should be no surprises — the feedback conversations should have happened months earlier.

Read full guide →
Calibration Sessions: Ensuring Fairness
Team Lead
+

Calibration sessions align performance ratings across managers to prevent grade inflation, bias, and inconsistency. Your "exceeds expectations" might be another manager's "meets expectations." Calibration forces the conversation: what evidence supports this rating, and is the standard being applied equally? It is uncomfortable — and essential for credibility of the entire performance system.

7.4 Strategic Leadership

Budgeting: Capex, Opex, and Headcount Planning
Project Mgr
+

Every GBS leader manages a budget — even if they do not realize it. Capex is capital expenditure (technology, infrastructure). Opex is operational expenditure (salaries, licenses, travel). Headcount planning converts business demand into FTE requirements and hiring timelines. Understanding these three dimensions is the gateway to being trusted with P&L responsibility.

Understanding the P&L for Non-Finance Managers
Project Mgr
+

A P&L (Profit and Loss statement) shows revenue, costs, and the margin between them. For GBS leaders, understanding where your team costs sit on the P&L — and how your improvements affect the bottom line — transforms you from a cost manager to a business partner. The CFO speaks P&L. If you want to be heard in that conversation, you need to speak it too.

Org Design: Span of Control and Layering
Project Mgr
+

Span of control is how many direct reports a manager has. Layering is how many management levels exist between the frontline and the VP. Too narrow a span creates expensive overhead. Too wide a span creates under-managed teams. Org design in GBS must balance cost efficiency with leadership effectiveness — and the answer varies by process complexity and team maturity.

Psychological Safety and Team Resilience
Team Lead
+

Psychological safety means team members can raise problems, ask questions, and admit mistakes without fear of punishment. It is the single strongest predictor of team performance in research. In GBS, where error rates are tracked and SLAs have consequences, creating safety is harder and more important. Teams that hide problems to avoid blame eventually produce the biggest failures.
