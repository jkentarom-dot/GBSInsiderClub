Career Path — Team Lead Level

Team Lead
Playbook

Yesterday you were one of them. Today you run the team. The skills that matter now are completely different from the ones that got you here — and nobody hands you a manual.

This is not a leadership theory course. The free tier covers theory. This path is the behavioral, political, and operational playbook for team leads who want to build a high-performing team, earn their authority, and position themselves for Manager — guided by an honest self-assessment and measured by a 90-day leadership roadmap.

Before You Start

How this path works

Self-paced study, real-job practice

Study 30-60 minutes daily. But the exercises happen with your actual team, your actual stakeholders, your actual manager. The 90-day milestones track real leadership behavior, not reading completion.

One behavior at a time

Each module targets one leadership behavior. Apply it the same week. Repeat until it becomes your default operating mode. Moving on before the previous behavior is habitual defeats the purpose.

AI in every module

Each exercise has an AI application using the three foundation techniques — Prompt Reversal, Session Document, Interview Mode. If your organization restricts AI use, every exercise has a manual alternative.

New to the foundation techniques? Read How to Learn & AI Foundations first — it applies to every path.

Career Booster — Step 1

Build a Leadership Mindset

The biggest shift isn't the title. It's the realization that your success is now measured by your team's output, not yours. Step 1 forces an honest assessment of where you stand as a leader — not where you stood as an individual contributor.

1.1Self-Assessment: Leadership Competencies — Evidence Only▾

The same rule applies: no example, no score. But the competencies have changed completely. You are no longer scored on how well you execute tasks. You are scored on how well your team delivers, how you handle people, and whether you display the capabilities your manager needs to see before trusting you with more.

2
Career Booster — Step 2

Understand Your Value & Perform

As a team lead, your value is measured differently. You don't deliver work — you deliver a team that delivers work. Step 2 covers the operational and people skills that define whether your team thrives or merely survives under your leadership.

2.1Understand What's Expected — Including What Nobody Says Out Loud▾

Your manager will give you targets: SLAs, KPIs, TAT. Those are the voiced expectations. The unvoiced ones are where new team leads get blindsided: people management quality, team morale, documentation currency, continuous improvement culture, process discipline, and — increasingly — driving efficiency. The expectation that you'll do the same work with fewer resources grows with every rung on the ladder.

The full expectation map

People managementHandle escalationsDevelop talent, address low performers, retain high performers

EfficiencyStay within headcountFind ways to do the same work with less — commercial thinking
CommunicationSend status updatesSpeak confidently to stakeholders, present to larger groups over time

Practical Exercise

Schedule a 30-minute conversation with your manager. Ask: "Beyond the dashboard metrics, what does success look like for me this year?" Document everything — especially what they mention casually. Those casual mentions are the real priorities.

AI Application

Give AI your role description and team context. Interview Mode: "What are the typical unvoiced expectations for a GBS team lead that managers assume but rarely articulate? Interview me about my situation, then build a complete expectation map."

I've had an expectations conversation with my manager beyond the KPIs

I know what "efficiency" means for my team — and where the potential sits
2.2The Talent Management Pivot — From Doing Work to Making People Successful▾

This is the single biggest capability shift. Before, you were measured on your own output. Now, your job is to attract, identify, develop, retain, and promote talent — to make them successful in delivering whatever your team owns. Many new team leads were great, reliable individual contributors. That skill set is necessary but no longer sufficient. Empathy matters, but so does the corporate reality: this is not a charity. Low performers create extra work, damage morale, and carry reputational risk for the entire department.

The talent cycle you now own
  • Attract — be the team lead people want to work for. Your reputation as a developer of people is your recruiting tool.
  • Identify — know who your high performers, steady performers, and struggling performers are. Know why.
  • Develop — invest time in each person's growth. 1:1s are development conversations, not status updates.
  • Retain — create an environment people don't want to leave, even for slightly more money elsewhere. Morale beats salary within a reasonable range.
  • Promote — actively advocate for your people when they're ready. Their career movement reflects on you.
From the Field

The best team leads Julian worked with kept misplaced hires low and dealt with performance issues early, positively, and professionally. The worst ones avoided hard conversations, let struggling team members drag the group down, and then acted surprised when their best people left — tired of carrying the load.

Practical Exercise

Map your team: for each person, note their current performance level, their development trajectory, and one specific action you can take this month to support their growth. If you can't name an action, that's the gap.

AI Application

Describe your team composition (anonymized). Ask AI to help build a talent development matrix: "For each profile, suggest one development action, one retention risk indicator, and one conversation I should be having."

I can categorize every team member by performance level with evidence
Each person has at least one development action this quarter
I've had one genuine development conversation this month — not a status update
2.3Delegation & the Micromanagement Trap▾

Micromanagement means: checking the work of associates excessively, giving "how" instructions instead of "what" (objectives or deliverables), overriding the way of working the associate prefers or proposes. It might be helpful for a new learner or an unknown task, but it's resented by an experienced associate. It creates a feeling of distrust — and that distrust spreads.

Context matters — this is not black and white

A new team lead learning the ropes, a team under pressure with inexperienced members, a process in crisis mode — hands-on management may be survival. The problem arises when it becomes the permanent management style. The test: if the pressure lifted tomorrow, would you still be checking everything? If yes, that's the habit to break.

If you're still performing your former associate job plus team lead tasks, you're almost certainly failing at one of them. It isn't fair if your manager left that situation unresolved — but you need to escalate it. Passion for the ground work is understandable; your team may even appreciate that you know their job. But your job is now "leadership and tell," not "do and manage."

The delegation framework
  • Define the "what" — the deliverable, the deadline, the quality standard
  • Let them own the "how" — unless they ask for guidance or the risk is too high
  • Check at milestones, not continuously — agree on checkpoints up front
  • Accept "different but acceptable" — their approach doesn't need to match yours
Practical Exercise

Identify one task you're still doing from your old role. Delegate it this week using the framework above. Document: what you delegated, to whom, what "what" you defined, and what happened. If you have no old-role tasks left, delegate one decision you currently make yourself.

AI Application

Describe your current task portfolio. Ask AI: "Which of these should a team lead NOT be doing? For each, draft a delegation brief: the deliverable, the quality standard, and the checkpoint schedule."

I've delegated at least one task using the "what not how" framework
I'm no longer performing my old associate role alongside TL duties
I can describe the difference between hands-on leadership and micromanagement
2.4The Hard Conversations — Low Performers, Team Tension, Requests▾

This module covers the conversations every team lead dreads — and the ones that define your leadership credibility. Three scenarios, one principle: handle them professionally, with empathy, but without avoiding the issue.

Scenario 1: The low performer

Associates perform on different levels. Some are not suitable, some need significantly more time. A good team lead addresses performance issues positively and early — first to diagnose: is this temporary (personal issues, adjustment period), a capability mismatch (right attitude, wrong role), or a behavioral problem (attitude, effort)?

DiagnosisResponseCommon mistake
Temporary / personalSupport, adjust expectations short-term, check in regularlyIgnoring it and hoping it resolves
Capability mismatchExplore redeployment to a role that fits — if the attitude is rightPutting them on a PIP when the job simply exceeds their capability
Behavioral / attitudeClear feedback, documented expectations, PIP if neededAvoiding the conversation until it's too late
Blind Spot

A PIP on someone who fundamentally cannot do the job isn't development — it's a legal formality for termination dressed as an opportunity. If the gap is capability, not effort, the humane path is redeployment or an honest conversation about fit. For the sake of the team's health and performance, a termination or redeployment is the right thing — the low performer creates extra work for everyone and reputational risk for the department. Do it humanely, fairly, and supportively.

Scenario 2: Salary and promotion requests

You will get these. The rules: never just grant a request — that creates a queue (this information travels fast among associates). Lay out the path: what needs to happen, by when, and how raises and promotions are earned within the HR and center strategy. Be transparent and fair.

The exception: a genuine high performer at the edge of leaving. An extraordinary increase may be cheaper and more reasonable than losing them — the cost of a new hire plus training plus ramp-up, especially in markets with inflation, often exceeds the cost of retention. Make this case to your manager if it applies. But the broader goal is creating an environment where people stay because they want to — not because of a counter-offer.

Scenario 3: Team tension and peer-to-boss dynamics

If you were promoted from within the team, some former peers will adjust naturally. Others won't. You hold the upper hand — and you hold the responsibility. Either take the lead in resolving it or, in serious cases, make the hard decision that the working relationship can't continue. Letting tension fester is the one choice that always makes things worse.

Practical Exercise

Identify the hardest people conversation you're currently avoiding. Prepare it using the diagnosis framework above. Set a date this week to have it — or, if the situation doesn't exist, prepare a "what I would say" brief for one of the three scenarios and review it with a mentor or peer TL.

AI Application

Role-play all three scenarios with AI as the team member. Try multiple approaches: "Play a low performer who gets defensive. Play an associate requesting a raise I can't grant. Play a former peer who doesn't accept my authority." Practice the words before the moment arrives.

I've had or prepared for one hard conversation using the diagnosis framework
I know the difference between a PIP that develops and a PIP that exits
I can handle a salary request without caving or dismissing — I lay out the path
2.5Skip-Level Meetings — Prep Your Team, Control the Narrative▾

Directors and VPs may schedule skip-level meetings — conversations directly with your team members, bypassing you. These can signal genuine interest in ground-level reality, or they can be information-gathering exercises. Either way, your job is the same: prepare your team so nobody is blindsided.

How to handle skip-levels
  • Inform the team in advance — visits and sessions are usually announced, but explain the format (organized agenda or informal drop-by)
  • Frame it positively, always — even if your gut says otherwise. Creating unnecessary fear in the team helps nobody.
  • For new joiners especially: explain what skip-levels are, that they're normal, and what to expect
  • Don't coach people on what to say — that backfires if discovered. Instead, build a team that has genuine good things to report because you're actually leading well.
Pro Tip

The best defense against a skip-level turning into a surprise is running a team where there are no surprises. If you're addressing problems, developing people, and creating a positive environment, skip-levels become endorsements, not risks.

I know my organization's skip-level culture — and I've prepared my team for it
New joiners understand what leadership visits look like in our center
3
Career Booster — Step 3

Build Your Authority & Visibility

Authority isn't automatic with the title. It's earned through how you carry yourself, how you treat your team, and how your team's reputation travels beyond your immediate scope. Step 3 is about building both — and avoiding the behaviors that destroy them overnight.

3.1Authority, Distance & Equal Treatment▾

With authority comes responsibility — and a change in how people relate to you. Former peers may treat you differently: less intimately, more carefully. That's normal. Your job is to accept the distance and make sure you treat every team member professionally and equally. Favoritism — real or perceived — is the fastest way to fracture a team.

The authority paradox

You need to be approachable enough that people bring you problems early, and authoritative enough that decisions stick. Too friendly: decisions get challenged, boundaries blur. Too distant: problems get hidden, morale drops. The balance is professional warmth — genuine interest in people combined with clear expectations and consistent follow-through.

Credit and blame — the rules
  • Team wins are your team's wins. Share credit explicitly and publicly. Name people.
  • Team failures are your failures. Take responsibility upward. Fix it with the team privately.
  • Taking credit for a team member's work is the single fastest way to lose trust — and the team talks. Always.
Practical Exercise

In your next team meeting or status update, publicly credit a team member by name for a specific contribution. Observe the reaction — from them and from the team. Make this a habit, not a one-time gesture.

I treat all team members with professional equality — and they'd confirm it
I've publicly credited a team member by name this month
I take responsibility for team failures upward and fix them with the team privately
3.2Speaking Up & Stakeholder Exposure▾

As a team lead, you report upward and converse with service recipients. Both audiences are judging your confidence, clarity, and composure — often unconsciously. Public speaking is a progression: team huddles this month, department presentations next quarter, stakeholder forums next year. The small occasions are the gym.

The progression
  • Run a structured team huddle or standup — own the format, keep it tight, drive outcomes
  • Present one operational update to your manager's peers or a stakeholder group
  • Volunteer for one cross-functional meeting where your team's work is discussed
  • Build toward: presenting at department-level town halls, leading stakeholder reviews
From the Field

Building trust with your team is the foundation — that's where service performance comes from. But your reputation outside your team is what gets you promoted. Your manager, your manager's peers, your service recipients — they all form impressions. Make sure those impressions come from your actual performance, not from silence.

Practical Exercise

Run or redesign your team's standup/huddle this week. Keep it under 15 minutes, action-oriented, no status recitals. Then identify one upcoming cross-functional meeting you can attend or present in.

AI Application

Give AI the context of your next presentation (audience, topic, time limit). Ask: "Structure this as a 3-part executive update: situation, actions, what I need from this group. Keep it under 5 minutes."

I run a structured team huddle with a consistent format
I've presented to at least one audience beyond my immediate team
My manager's peers and at least one stakeholder know my name and my team's work

Career Booster — Step 4

Acquire Next-Level Skills

The gap from Team Lead to Manager is wider than most people think. Managers think in P&L, handle multi-team complexity, display corporate policy fluency, and manage stakeholders who outrank them. Step 4 starts building those capabilities while you still have the safety net of a single team.

4.1The Manager Gap — What's Actually Different▾

A team lead runs a team. A manager runs a function — or multiple teams. The differences aren't just scale. They're category changes in how you think, what you're accountable for, and who you interact with.

DimensionTeam LeadManager
ScopeOne team, one process areaMultiple teams, cross-functional scope
MetricsSLAs, KPIs, team performanceP&L, cost-to-serve, business outcomes
StakeholdersDirect manager, service recipientsDirectors, VPs, external partners, steering committees
People decisionsPerformance, delegation, team dynamicsOrg design, headcount planning, succession across teams
Strategic inputImprovement suggestionsTechnology roadmaps, location strategy, operating model decisions
Authority displayWithin teamAcross organization — corporate policy fluency, negotiation with senior stakeholders

Having had TL roles in different teams strengthens your Manager candidacy — it shows versatility and reduces the risk that your leadership only works in one context.

Practical Exercise

Shadow your manager for one week — pay attention to what they do that you don't: the meetings they attend, the conversations they have, the decisions they make. Document 5 activities that don't exist in your role today.

AI Application

Upload both your TL job description and the Manager JD. Ask: "Build a gap analysis between these two roles. For each gap, suggest one action I can take in my current TL position to start building that capability."

I can describe 5 things a Manager does that I don't do today
I've shadowed or observed my manager's scope for at least one week
I have a gap table with specific actions for my top 3 Manager-level gaps
4.2Financial & Commercial Literacy▾

Managers think in P&L. Team leads who don't understand budgets, headcount economics, and cost-to-serve are speaking a different language from the people who decide promotions. You don't need to be a finance expert — you need to understand how the numbers behind your team work.

What you need to know
  • Your team's cost — headcount cost, fully loaded (salary + benefits + overhead + seat cost)
  • Cost-to-serve — what it costs to process one transaction, one ticket, one case in your area
  • The efficiency expectation — doing the same or more work with fewer FTEs over time. This is the commercial reality of GBS.
  • Capex vs Opex basics — what gets capitalized (projects, implementations) vs. what's operational expense (your team's salary)
  • Headcount decisions — how backfills are approved, how new positions are justified, what a business case looks like
Practical Exercise

Ask your manager: "Can you walk me through our team's budget and cost structure?" If they can't or won't share specifics, ask for the concepts — what drives cost, what drives headcount decisions, what "efficiency" means in practice.

AI Application

Ask AI to build a sample team budget for a 10-person GBS team in your location. Validate the assumptions against your reality. Ask: "What would a headcount business case look like if I needed to justify one additional FTE?"

I can explain my team's cost-to-serve in one sentence
I understand how headcount decisions are made in my organization
I can articulate the efficiency expectation — and where I see potential
4.3Succession & Freedom — Make Yourself Unnecessary▾

The most underrated skill at team lead level: identify and develop a successor early, plus a backup if the preferred candidate leaves. This sounds counterintuitive — why train your replacement? Because it's the only way to free yourself for the next opportunity.

The succession paradox

If nobody can replace you, you can't be promoted. If your team falls apart when you're on leave, your manager sees fragility, not indispensability. The team leads who got promoted fastest in Julian's experience were the ones whose teams ran smoothly without them — because they'd built the layer behind them.

The strategy
  • Identify 1-2 potential successors — people with the aptitude and willingness
  • Gradually delegate real TL tasks to them: running huddles, handling escalations, owning a workstream
  • Build substitutes: who covers when you're on leave, and how prepared are they?
  • Redesign processes so they don't depend on your personal knowledge
  • Use the runway you've created to work on next-level skills: attend manager meetings, take on cross-functional work, substitute for your manager
From the Field

Manage your team so well that they operate without your involvement. Empower, redesign, build the layer behind you. That runway is exactly how you take over more responsibility — including substituting for your manager and gaining direct exposure to a role one level above yours.

Practical Exercise

Name your successor candidate. This week, delegate one TL-level task to them with proper context. If you don't have a candidate, that's the finding — start developing one. Document your succession plan, even if informal.

AI Application

Describe your team structure. Ask: "Help me build a succession plan: who could be my backup, what tasks should I start delegating to them, and what's the 90-day development path to make them ready?"

I've identified at least one successor candidate
I've delegated one TL-level task to them with proper context
My team can operate for a week without me — and I've tested it

Career Booster — Step 5

Execute

You've built the team, earned the authority, and started operating at the next level. Step 5 turns all of that into a promotion case — or a clear-eyed plan for what to do if the timing isn't right.

5.1The Brag Sheet — Team Lead Edition▾

Your Brag Sheet at TL level tracks two categories: your team's achievements (which reflect your leadership) and your personal capability demonstrations (which signal Manager readiness). Every team milestone, every hard conversation you handled well, every efficiency initiative, every time a team member was promoted under your leadership — documented, dated, with outcomes.

Practical Exercise

Update your Brag Sheet with the provided TL template. Minimum 15 entries before your tollgate — split across team achievements and personal leadership demonstrations. Each entry: what happened, what you did, the result, who witnessed it.

AI Application

Feed AI your Brag Sheet and the Manager JD: "Map my achievements against Manager competencies. Where am I strong? Where do I need more evidence? Suggest what kind of achievement would fill each gap."

5.2The Promotion Case — From Team Lead to Manager▾

The promotion conversation isn't "I want to be a Manager." It's "here's the evidence I'm already operating at Manager level — here's what I've built, here's what I've delivered, here's the gap analysis I've done, and here's my succession plan for my current role. What's the path and timeline?"

Your case document includes
  • Team performance trajectory under your leadership — improvement trend, not one good quarter
  • People development outcomes — who you've grown, promoted, or retained
  • Financial literacy demonstrated — cost-to-serve awareness, efficiency initiatives
  • Stakeholder relationships beyond your team — who knows you and your work
  • Succession plan — who replaces you and how ready they are
  • Manager-level activities already performed — substituting, cross-functional work, strategic input
Practical Exercise

Build your promotion case document using the template provided. Rehearse it with a mentor or peer — then with AI as a skeptical manager. Refine until every claim has evidence.

AI Application

AI role-play as a budget-constrained manager: "I'm presenting my case for promotion to Manager. Challenge every claim. Ask for evidence. Push back on anything that sounds like ambition without substance."

I have a promotion case document with evidence for every claim
I've rehearsed the conversation — and can handle pushback
My succession plan is documented — I'm not irreplaceable, I'm promotable
5.3The Plateau Pattern & Plan B▾

Not every team lead becomes a manager — and not every team lead who deserves it gets promoted on their timeline. The reasons: missing next-level skills, stronger internal candidates, timing and organizational politics, or simply personal preference of decision-makers overriding the skill comparison. Being at the right place at the right time matters, despite your capabilities.

What you control
  • Building genuine Manager-level skills — not just asking for the title
  • Gaining trust with the people who decide — a long game, not a campaign
  • Diversifying your TL experience — leading different teams signals versatility
  • Developing corporate development programs — most organizations offer leadership courses at various levels. Use them.
What you do if the answer is "not yet"

Same principle as the Associate path: "not yet" with a concrete development plan and timeline is a good outcome — hold them to it. "No" without a path is information. The disciplined route: secure a real alternative, then decide.

Blind Spot

Sometimes the issue isn't your skills at all — it's that there are simply more candidates than positions, and personal preference or political dynamics tip the scale. That's not failure. That's organizational reality. The response isn't bitterness — it's building so much capability that the next opportunity, here or elsewhere, becomes inevitable.

I understand why TLs plateau — and which factors I can influence
I have a concrete Plan B if promotion timing doesn't align
