Career Path — Team Lead Level

Team Lead
Playbook

Yesterday you were one of them. Today you run the team. The talent management, authority, political, and operational skills that determine whether you advance to Manager or plateau.

The free tier gives you the frameworks. This path is the behavioral playbook for what changes when you become the person responsible for other people's performance, careers, and the team's output. Guided by the Career Chessboard, structured by the 5-Step Career Booster, measured by a 90-day roadmap with a real tollgate.

Before You Start

How this path works

Self-paced study, fixed-pace practice

Study 30-60 minutes daily. The practical exercises happen at your real job on a real roadmap. The 90-day milestones keep you honest. You're managing people now — the work can't wait.

One behavior per week

Each module ends in something you do with your team. Pick one leadership behavior, apply it, repeat until natural. Reading about delegation doesn't make you a delegator.

AI in every module

Each exercise has an AI application using Prompt Reversal, Session Document, and Interview Mode. If your organization restricts AI, every exercise has a manual alternative. Many exercises involve real conversations — AI prepares you, reality tests you.

Career Booster — Step 1

Build a Career Mindset

The assessment changes at Team Lead level. You're no longer scored on individual execution — you're scored on whether your team delivers and whether you're developing the capabilities a Manager needs. Different game, different scoreboard.

1.1Self-Assessment: The Team Lead Scoreboard▾

As an associate, the question was "are you good at your job?" As a Team Lead, the question splits: "does your team deliver?" and "could you run something bigger?" Rate yourself against each competency below. Same rule applies: no example, no score.

CompetencyEvidence question

AccountabilityWhen someone misses a target, do you address it the same week — or let it drift?

Team BuildingHave you hired, onboarded, or developed someone who's now performing above expectations?

CompetencyEvidence question
Financial LiteracyCan you explain your team's cost-to-serve, and would your number be close to your manager's?

Reality Check

The shift is real: strong individual contributors can score low here. Direction, accountability, and team building are learned skills, not personality traits. If your Impact scores are weak, the path starts there — managing people well is the prerequisite to everything that follows.

AI Application

Upload a Team Lead or Supervisor job description for your organization. Ask: "Based on this JD, build me an evidence-based self-assessment. For each factor, ask me for a concrete example involving my team before assigning a score."

1.2The Career Chessboard — Team Lead Edition▾

Same framework, different competencies. Your eight scores collapse into Impact (team delivery) and Readiness (manager capabilities). The square you land on drives your focus for the entire path.

The "Super-IC" square is the most common trap for promoted associates. High personal output, low team development. Module 2.2 addresses this directly.

AI Application

Give AI your eight scores and your square. Ask: "Interview me about my team context, then tell me which two competencies, if improved, would move my position fastest — and what my manager would need to see as evidence."

1.3Set Your Ambition — What Are You Building Toward?▾

The path adapts to your honest answer. At Team Lead level, the ambition question sharpens — the roles ahead are fewer, more competitive, and require distinctly different skills.

IntentWhat it meansHow the path adjusts

From the Field

The TL-to-Manager gap is wider than it looks. What the next level actually requires:

  • Stronger stakeholder management — more senior, more demanding, more political.
  • Comfort with complexity across multiple teams, not depth in one.
  • Fluency in corporate policy and the ability to display authority.
  • Breadth of experience — having led different teams, not just one, significantly strengthens your case.

Be realistic about the timeline: if there are stronger candidates or no open positions, skills alone won't do it. Timing, trust, and sometimes politics matter too.

Career Booster — Step 2

Master Your Role & Team Performance

The core pivot: from "doing the work" to "making others successful at doing the work." This is where most team leads either grow or get stuck. Every module here is a skill your team feels directly.

2.1The Talent Management Pivot — Your Actual Job Now▾

Your job description changed. You're no longer measured on how well you execute tasks — you're measured on whether you can attract, identify, develop, retain, and promote talent that delivers what the team owns. Many team leads were great individual contributors. That's irrelevant now. The question is: can you make other people great?

The talent cycle you now own
StageWhat it means at TL level
AttractHelp hiring managers identify the right profiles. Know what "good" looks like for your team's work.
IdentifySpot high potential early. Know who's coasting, who's growing, who's struggling.
DevelopCoaching, stretch assignments, feedback loops. Not "send them to training" — active development.
RetainCreate an environment people don't want to leave. Morale, growth opportunities, fairness.
PromoteAdvocate for your people's careers. A TL who develops future TLs is worth more than one who hoards talent.

Empathy matters — understanding what your people need, reading situations, sensing tension before it surfaces. But empathy alone isn't leadership. This isn't a charity. Low performers need to be dealt with, tension needs resolution, and professional standards need enforcement. The skill is doing both: caring about people while holding the line.

Practical Exercise

Map your team: for each person, write one sentence on where they are in the talent cycle (developing, performing, at risk, ready for more). Identify one person you should invest more time in and one situation you've been avoiding. Act on both this week.

AI Application

Describe your team composition (anonymized). Ask: "Interview me about each person's performance and potential. Then help me build a talent map with specific development actions — not generic 'provide feedback,' but actions I can take this month."

Session Recap — Why it matters

Your real output as a TL is people who grow and deliver — you can't manage what you haven't mapped.

I can describe each team member's position in the talent cycle
I have at least one active development action per person
I've addressed one situation I was avoiding
2.2Hands-On vs. Hands-Off — When to Step In, When to Let Go▾

Micromanagement has a definition: checking finished work, giving "how" instructions instead of "what" objectives, overriding the way an associate prefers to work. It might be helpful for a new joiner on an unfamiliar task. It's destructive as a permanent management style — it signals distrust and prevents people from developing ownership.

Context matters — read the situation

A new TL in a high-pressure team with inexperienced associates may need to be hands-on as a survival tactic. That's not micromanagement — it's crisis management. The issue arises when the crisis passes and the behavior doesn't. If you're still checking every output and dictating every step six months in, you haven't transitioned — you're still doing your old job with a new title.

The IC addiction trap

If you're performing your former role plus team lead tasks, you're failing at one of them — likely both. The honest diagnosis:

  • Sometimes it's structural — delayed backfill, capacity gaps. That may be your manager's fault, but escalating it with data is your responsibility.
  • Sometimes it's you — you enjoy the ground work. Recognize the cost: your team is either overloaded or neglected while you do their job.
  • The role is now leadership and direction, not execution. A hands-on TL earns appreciation — but appreciation doesn't develop anyone's capabilities.

Blind Spot

Corporations offer internal leadership development programs and courses for various levels. Use them. Most TLs discover skills they thought were intuitive actually need to be learned — delegation, feedback, performance conversations. The programs exist because the transition is hard for everyone, not just you.

Practical Exercise

Track your time for one week: what percentage is leadership (coaching, planning, reviewing, stakeholder management) vs. execution (doing work your team should do)? If execution exceeds 30%, identify what you need to delegate or escalate as a capacity issue.

AI Application

Describe your weekly activities. Ask: "Categorize each as 'leadership' or 'execution.' For every execution task, tell me who on my team could do it, what training they'd need, and draft a delegation conversation."

Session Recap — Why it matters

Doing your old job plus the TL job means failing at both. The ratio has to shift on purpose.

I know my leadership-to-execution time ratio
I've delegated at least one task I was holding onto
I've enrolled in or identified an internal leadership development program
2.3Managing Low Performers — The Escalation Ladder▾

Every team has performance variation. Some associates need more time. Some are misplaced. Some have temporary personal issues affecting output. Your job is to diagnose the root cause before choosing the response — and to act, not wait. A low performer left unaddressed creates extra work for the team and reputational risk for the department. The team sees inaction. It erodes trust.

The diagnostic — why before what
Root causeIndicatorsResponse
Temporary / personalPreviously solid performer, recent decline, external signs of stressSupport, adjust workload temporarily, check in privately. Set a timeframe for recovery.
Capability gapWilling and trying, but the role requirements exceed their skillsTraining if the gap is closeable. Redeployment to a better-fit role if the attitude is right but the capability fundamentally isn't.
Behavioral / attitudeCapable but unwilling — misses standards, disrupts team, resists feedbackFormal feedback, documented expectations, PIP if necessary. A PIP is a structured last chance, not a formality.
Reality Check

A common mistake: putting someone on a PIP when the job requirements completely exceed their capability. A PIP assumes the gap is closeable with effort. If it isn't, the PIP becomes a legal formality for termination — a "structured exit" disguised as a development plan. That's not fair to anyone. If the person's attitude is right but the fit is wrong, redeployment is the humane and honest path. If termination is the right call, do it as fairly and supportively as possible — for the team's health and the individual's dignity.

Practical Exercise

Identify any current performance concern on your team — even a mild one. Diagnose the root cause using the table above. Document what you've observed (specific, factual, recent). Have the conversation this week using the professional approach: facts, impact, support offered, expectations set.

AI Application

Describe the situation (anonymized). Ask: "Help me diagnose whether this is temporary, a capability gap, or behavioral. Then draft the conversation — what to say, what to listen for, and what to document afterwards."

Session Recap — Why it matters

Performance problems compound in silence. Early, honest, documented beats late and messy.

I have no unaddressed performance concerns older than 30 days
I can distinguish temporary, capability, and behavioral root causes
I've had at least one structured performance conversation

Word-for-Word — The Capability Conversation (redeployment)

"How are you feeling today? I've observed you're still struggling — I see you staying late just to catch up. I genuinely feel bad that you're having such a hard time, and I have to be honest with you: I see it continuing."

"Please understand that I have to step in — for the sake of your own health, and for the team that has to absorb the overflow. I've decided to take you off this role, and we'd like to offer you a position in [team], which we believe is a much better fit."

"I know this might come as a shock. Believe me, this is the better path forward. Let's meet tomorrow to go through the details of the new role and your decision."

Why it works

  • Opens with observed facts and genuine concern — not accusation. The person already knows they're drowning; naming it kindly removes the pretense.
  • States the decision clearly ('I've decided') — no false negotiation that drags out the pain.
  • Frames the move around their health AND team impact — both are true, and both protect their dignity.
  • Ends with a concrete next step and gives them a night to process. Shock needs time, not more talking.

Word-for-Word — The Behavioral Conversation (PIP)

"We're continuing to see a decline in your performance in the KPI report. I thought we were aligned in our last conversation that this couldn't continue — and I've given you time to sort out your situation and get back on track."

"As of today, I'm placing you on a formal Performance Improvement Plan. It lays out exactly the performance we expect and the actions required from your side."

"Please ask for whatever help you need to get this back under control — and let's meet on [date] to review your progress."

Why it works

  • References the prior conversation and the time already given — the PIP is never the first conversation, and the script makes that history explicit.
  • Data leads ('the KPI report'), not feelings. Behavioral cases are won or lost on documentation.
  • The PIP is announced as a decision, paired immediately with an offer of support — accountability and help in the same breath.
  • A fixed review date makes it real. No date, no plan.

Quick check

A capable team member with a great attitude is in the wrong seat and struggling. Is a PIP the right tool?

No. A PIP assumes the gap is closeable with effort. When the attitude is right but the fit is wrong, redeployment to a better-fitting role is the humane and honest path. A PIP here would just be a slow, painful exit for someone who could thrive elsewhere.
2.4The Authority Shift — Peer to Boss, Equal Treatment, Professional Distance▾

Authority arrives with the title — but acceptance doesn't. You may have been peers with people you now evaluate. The relationships change, and pretending they don't creates worse problems than acknowledging the shift. You'll be treated differently: less intimate, more observed, more judged. That's the role, not a problem to solve.

The non-negotiables
  • Treat every team member with equal professionalism — no favorites, no grudges from peer days
  • Decisions must be explainable. "Because I said so" works exactly zero times in a professional setting.
  • Maintain enough distance that your judgments aren't questioned for personal bias — but enough closeness that trust exists
  • If a former-peer conflict runs deep, you hold the upper hand. Resolve it actively or — if irrecoverable — make the hard call. Letting it fester poisons the team.
Practical Exercise

Audit your relationships: is there anyone on your team you treat differently — more leniently or more harshly — because of your history as peers? Write down the specific behavior, then correct it this week. Ask a trusted colleague outside your team for a reality check if you're unsure.

AI Application

Describe a peer-to-boss transition scenario. Ask: "Role-play as the team member who's uncomfortable with the new dynamic. Help me practice three different responses — one too distant, one too friendly, one professional."

Session Recap — Why it matters

The moment the team senses favouritism, trust and information flow stop. Equal treatment is non-negotiable.

I treat all team members with demonstrably equal professionalism
I've addressed any lingering peer-era dynamics
My team knows what to expect from me — consistency, not surprises

Word-for-Word — When a Former Peer Calls You Out ('You used to do the same thing')

"Thanks for reminding me. I was wrong — and now it's time to do it right."

Why it works

  • Zero defensiveness — the attack has nothing to push against, so it ends in one sentence.
  • Owning the past mistake openly costs nothing; your authority comes from the standard you set now, not from a perfect history.
  • It converts a challenge to your authority into a demonstration of it: secure leaders admit error, insecure ones argue.
  • The team hears the real message: the standard applies to everyone — including the version of you that came before.

Likability Lab — The Pratfall Effect

The science: Psychologist Elliot Aronson found that competent people become MORE likable after admitting a small blunder — imperfection humanizes capability. The effect only works when competence is already established; it's a leader's tool, not an excuse.

Try this: When you're wrong in front of the team, say so in one plain sentence and move to the fix. Never explain at length, never counter-attack. A 10-second admission buys more trust than a 10-minute justification.

Field Skills

“How do I deal with a really difficult manager?”

First, protect yourself and keep your professionalism intact — your reputation is built on how you handle this, not on winning. A measured approach:

  • Keep delivering and document — consistently strong work removes any legitimate criticism, and a factual record of interactions (dates, what was said, decisions) protects you if things escalate.
  • Raise it constructively first — neutral, solution-focused language: “I've noticed some friction in our workflow — I'd like to find a way to work better together.” Focus on the situation, not the person.
  • Set professional boundaries — especially around after-hours demands, restated calmly and in writing.
  • Escalate appropriately when needed — if behavior crosses into disrespect, discrimination, or genuinely affects your health or work, that is a conversation for HR or a trusted senior leader. Bring your documentation, not gossip.

You don't have to simply endure a genuinely toxic situation. Protecting your wellbeing and using the proper channels is the professional path — and if the environment can't be fixed, it's also legitimate to plan a move.

If the situation is affecting your wellbeing, talk to someone you trust — a mentor, HR, or a professional. Looking after your health is never a sign of weakness.

2.5CI Culture & Process Discipline — Run It Like a Business▾

As a TL, you own the culture of continuous improvement in your team. That means process discipline: documentation stays current, SOPs reflect reality, errors are tracked and addressed, and the team looks for ways to do the same work better — or the same work with fewer resources. This is where commercial acumen starts: not in a finance course, but in the daily habit of asking "is this efficient?"

What "CI culture" looks like in practice
  • SOPs and process documentation reviewed quarterly — assigned, not assumed
  • Errors tracked, root-caused, and fixed — not just reported
  • Team members empowered to flag waste and suggest improvements — and recognized when they do
  • A visible improvement pipeline: what are we working on, what's completed, what's the impact?
  • Efficiency mindset: can we deliver this with fewer steps, fewer handoffs, fewer exceptions?
Practical Exercise

Pick one process in your area. Audit: is the SOP current? Are errors tracked? Is there one improvement you could implement in 2 weeks? Execute it. Document the before-and-after for your own portfolio and your team's visibility.

AI Application

Describe your team's main process. Ask: "Identify the 3 most likely waste categories in this process. For each, give me a specific question to ask my team to surface real examples — and a template to track and resolve them."

Session Recap — Why it matters

Process discipline is what lets you step away and step up. Undocumented work traps you.

My team's SOPs are current — or I've assigned an update schedule
Errors in my area are tracked with root causes, not just counts
I've implemented or initiated at least one process improvement

Career Booster — Step 3

Build Visibility & Team Reputation

At TL level, visibility shifts from personal to team. Your reputation is your team's performance. The fastest way to damage it is taking credit for their work. The fastest way to build it is making your team's achievements known — with their names attached.

3.1Building Your Team's Reputation — Credit Flows Down▾

A TL who takes credit for team members' work destroys trust faster than any other mistake. The team notices immediately, and the damage compounds:

  • People stop going above and beyond — why would they?
  • Information stops flowing upward — you hear about problems last.
  • Your best people start looking elsewhere — quietly, then suddenly.

The rule is simple: credit flows down, accountability flows up. Your team's wins are their wins, named individually. Your team's failures are your responsibility.

Create an environment worth staying in

Retention isn't about blocking exits — it's about making the team a place people choose to be. Fair treatment, growth opportunities, recognition, and the sense that their TL has their back. When associates can get slightly more money elsewhere but choose to stay — that's your leadership working. When they leave despite liking the work — look at what you're not providing.

Practical Exercise

In your next report or update to leadership, mention a team member by name for a specific contribution. Make it a habit: one named recognition per week, in a visible channel. Track whether engagement shifts over 30 days.

Session Recap — Why it matters

Credit flows down. Named recognition is the cheapest, strongest retention tool you have.

I consistently credit team members by name in upward communication
I can identify what keeps each person on my team — and what might push them out

Likability Lab — The Gratitude Multiplier

The science: Researchers Adam Grant and Francesca Gino found that a single expression of sincere thanks made people significantly more likely to help again — gratitude signals their effort was seen, and being seen drives effort.

Try this: Make recognition named, specific, and public: "Priya caught the duplicate-payment pattern before it hit the client" beats "great job, team" every time. One specific public thank-you per week is a retention tool that costs nothing.

3.2Public Speaking & Communication Progression▾

As a TL, you speak to your team daily. As a Manager, you'll speak to departments, stakeholders, and leadership. The progression is deliberate: team huddles this quarter, department presentations next quarter, larger forums after that. Start small, build the muscle. Communication is trainable — it's not a personality trait you either have or don't.

The progression ladder
  • Run structured team huddles — consistent format, clear purpose, timed
  • Present your team's results in a manager-level meeting
  • Deliver one stakeholder update or service review
  • Volunteer for one cross-team or department-level presentation
Skip-level readiness

Leaders — Directors, VPs, your manager's manager — may visit your team or run skip-level meetings. These can be organized or improvised. Your job: prep your team positively. Frame it as genuine interest from leadership, never as surveillance. For new joiners, explain what skip-levels look like and why they happen. Control the narrative before anxiety fills the vacuum.

Practical Exercise

Run one structured team huddle this week using a consistent format (5-10 minutes, 3 topics: yesterday's highlights, today's priorities, blockers). Volunteer to present one result at the next manager-level meeting.

AI Application

Ask AI to design a team huddle format for your context — team size, process type, time zone spread. Then: "Draft talking points for a 5-minute presentation of my team's last month to my manager's leadership meeting."

Session Recap — Why it matters

Rhythm and clarity are how a team runs without you in every detail. Structure buys you headroom.

I run regular structured team huddles
I've presented my team's results at least once at a higher-level meeting
My team knows what to expect from skip-level meetings
3.3Handling Salary & Promotion Requests — Keep the Fort▾

You will get asked for raises and promotions. The wrong response — giving in to avoid conflict — creates a queue: information travels fast, and every associate who hears about it will expect the same. The right response: lay out the path, align it with HR and center strategy, and make it fair, transparent, and achievable for high performers.

How to handle the conversation
  • Acknowledge the ask — don't dismiss or deflect. The person took a risk by asking.
  • Explain the rules: when raises happen, what criteria are used, what the cycle looks like. No surprises.
  • If they deserve it: advocate upward. Build the case with evidence. That's your job.
  • If they're not ready: be honest about the gap. Offer a concrete development plan with a timeline. "Not yet, and here's how to get there" is a good outcome.
From the Field

The exception: a genuine high performer at the edge of leaving. In that case, an extraordinary retention package may be cheaper and smarter than replacement, training, and ramp-up — especially in markets where inflation makes new hires expensive. Know the math. Present it to your manager with the business case, not the emotional case. These decisions are often above your authority — your job is to escalate with the right data.

Practical Exercise

Learn your organization's compensation and promotion rules: cycle timing, criteria, approval chain, budget constraints. Document them in a one-page reference you can use in conversations. If you don't know — ask HR or your manager now, before the request comes.

AI Application

Role-play the conversation: "You're a solid performer asking me for a raise. I need to explain the path without making promises I can't keep. Coach me through three versions: the associate is early, the associate deserves it, and the associate is threatening to leave."

Session Recap — Why it matters

You can't fairly handle a salary or promotion request if you don't know the rules you're working inside.

I know my organization's compensation and promotion rules
I can handle a salary/promotion request with professionalism and honesty
I've advocated for at least one team member's development or recognition

Word-for-Word — The Promotion Request You Can't Grant (strong performer, no open role)

"Thanks for coming to me with your promotion request — I mean that. As we discussed last time, there's no possibility within this team right now. But I took your case to HR and to the other managers, to see whether a role is opening in the near future that fits your skills and interests."

"We haven't found a suitable role yet — and I want to be straight about that rather than keep you hoping. What I can tell you is that we want to keep you on this team, and we'll keep looking."

"In the meantime: there's a leadership course coming up that I'd like to nominate you for — a first solid step in this direction. And there are leadership tasks I can start handing you that build exactly the skills you'll need when the role does open."

Why it works

  • Thanks them for asking directly — it reinforces the behavior you want (signal interest the right way) instead of punishing it.
  • Shows action taken on their behalf ('I took your case to HR and the other managers') — the answer is no, but they were represented, not dismissed.
  • Honest about the gap. False hope is the retention killer, not the truth.
  • Replaces the unavailable title with concrete development: a course plus real leadership tasks. The path exists even when the role doesn't — that's what keeps strong people from walking.

Quick check

A strong performer asks for a promotion, but there's no open role. What's the trap to avoid?

Don't let yourself become a messenger carrying an unbacked request upward, and don't let it turn into a leaving-threat dynamic. Thank them for asking, be honest that no role exists yet, show you've genuinely explored it, and offer development now — a leadership course, stretch tasks. Keep them growing while the demand catches up.

Career Booster — Step 4

Build Next-Level Capabilities

Managers think differently than Team Leads. They think in P&L, not just SLA. They manage multiple teams, not one. They design organizations, not just operate them. Step 4 builds these capabilities while you're still in the TL seat — because they must be visible before you get the title.

4.1Financial Literacy & Commercial Acumen▾

A Team Lead manages work. A Manager manages a budget. The gap starts here: understanding cost-to-serve, headcount economics, and the difference between Capex and Opex. You don't need an MBA — you need to understand the numbers your manager uses to make decisions, and to think in efficiency terms: can we deliver the same service quality with fewer resources, fewer steps, fewer exceptions?

The financial vocabulary you need
  • Cost-to-serve: what does it cost to process one transaction, handle one ticket, close one case?
  • FTE economics: salary, benefits, overhead, training cost, ramp-up cost. Know the real number.
  • Capex vs. Opex: why it matters for project funding and headcount decisions
  • The "same work, fewer FTEs" conversation: efficiency gains fund growth and protect headcount during cuts
Practical Exercise

Ask your manager: "What's our team's cost-to-serve?" If they share the number, understand how it's calculated. If they don't have it, that's an opportunity — build a rough estimate together. Understanding your team's economics is a Manager-level skill displayed at TL level.

AI Application

Give AI your team size, location, and function. Ask: "Build me a basic cost-to-serve model. Walk me through each component so I understand what drives the number — and where efficiency gains would have the most impact."

Session Recap — Why it matters

Commercial awareness is what separates a supervisor from a future manager. Know the numbers.

I know my team's approximate cost-to-serve
I understand the FTE economics — what each person costs beyond salary
I've identified at least one efficiency opportunity with financial impact
4.2Succession Planning — Create Your Own Runway▾

The most underrated skill at TL level: identify and develop your successor early, plus a backup if the preferred one leaves. Without this, you're trapped. Every opportunity that requires you to step away gets blocked because nobody can cover your role. With succession in place, you create the runway to take on additional responsibilities, substitute for your manager, and eventually step into a bigger role.

The succession equation
  • Identify 1-2 people who could cover your role — not "someday," but within 3-6 months of focused development
  • Develop them actively: delegate decision-making, not just tasks. Let them run the team in your absence.
  • Build a backup in case your primary successor moves or leaves — the bus factor applies to your succession too
  • Document the undocumented: the decisions, relationships, and knowledge that only exist in your head
From the Field

The best strategy for gaining experience beyond your team: manage your team so well that they operate without your involvement. Empower, redesign processes, build substitutes behind you. The runway you create is the runway you use — to substitute for your manager, take over a second team temporarily, or lead a cross-functional initiative. Nobody promotes a single point of failure.

Practical Exercise

Name your successor candidate. Write down the three capabilities they'd need to cover your role. Start developing one of those capabilities this month — through delegation, coaching, or stretch assignment. Plan your first "TL-free day" where the successor runs the team.

AI Application

Ask: "I want to develop a succession plan for my TL role. Interview me about my team and responsibilities, then help me build a 90-day development plan for my successor candidate — with specific milestones and decision rights I can gradually transfer."

Session Recap — Why it matters

Without a successor you're trapped — every opportunity to step up gets blocked because nobody can cover you.

I have a named successor candidate (plus a backup)
My successor has taken ownership of at least one TL responsibility
I've had at least one "TL-free" period where the team ran without me
4.3Multi-Team Thinking & Org Design▾

Managers run multiple teams or an entire function. To get there, you need to think beyond your own team's boundaries: how does your team's work connect to adjacent teams? Where are the handoff inefficiencies? Could the teams be structured differently? These are questions your manager thinks about — and the TL who starts thinking about them gets noticed.

Org design thinking at TL level
  • Identify project resources: who on your team could support initiatives without breaking BAU?
  • Propose solutions: if you see a structural problem (wrong team boundaries, missing roles, skill gaps), draft a recommendation — even if the decision isn't yours
  • Understand span of control: how many direct reports is optimal for your work type? Why?
  • Cross-team collaboration: build relationships with adjacent TLs. Shared problems are often structural, not personal.
Practical Exercise

Map your team's interfaces: who do you hand work to, who hands work to you, where do things break? Write a one-page "how I'd restructure this" proposal — not to present necessarily, but to practice thinking at the function level.

AI Application

Describe your team and its adjacent teams. Ask: "Identify structural inefficiencies in how these teams interact. Propose 2-3 org design alternatives with trade-offs for each."

Career Booster — Step 5

Execute

You've built the team, developed the capabilities, and created the runway. Step 5 turns evidence into action — the promotion case, the conversation, and the honest assessment of what happens if the answer is "not yet."

5.1The Brag Sheet — Team Lead Edition▾

At TL level, the Brag Sheet shifts from personal achievements to team outcomes you enabled. A process you improved, a person you developed who got promoted, a crisis your team navigated, a stakeholder relationship you turned around. The evidence is about impact through others — which is exactly what a Manager does at scale.

Practical Exercise

Update your Brag Sheet with at least 10 entries. Each entry: what the team achieved, your specific leadership contribution, the measurable result, and who witnessed it. Include at least 3 entries about developing people — not just delivering results.

AI Application

Feed AI your Brag Sheet and the Manager job description. Ask: "Map my TL achievements against Manager competencies. Where am I strong? Where's the gap? Turn the strengths into 3 talking points for a promotion conversation."

5.2The Promotion Case & The Plateau Pattern▾

The TL-to-Manager promotion is competitive. The gap is wider than Associate-to-TL: you need demonstrated stakeholder management, comfort with multi-team complexity, versatility in corporate policy, and ideally TL experience in different teams — not just one. Your promotion case must show these, not just strong team delivery.

Why good TLs get stuck
  • Missing next-level skills they haven't acquired — financial literacy, strategic thinking, stakeholder influence
  • Stronger candidates exist — more versatile, more exposure, better-known across the organization
  • Timing and availability — the right role hasn't opened, or opened when they weren't ready
  • Personal preference and trust — leadership sometimes favors candidates they know better, regardless of capability. This is reality, not conspiracy.

If the answer is "not yet" with a concrete plan and timeline — hold them to it. If the answer is "no" without a path — that's information. The disciplined route: build your case, secure a genuine alternative if one exists, and make the decision with leverage and clarity.

Reality Check

Being at the right place at the right time matters — despite your skills. Trust is part of the game and it takes time to build. If there are multiple candidates, objective skill comparison isn't always what decides it. Focus on what you can control: skills, evidence, relationships, and readiness. The rest is positioning and patience.

Practical Exercise

Build your promotion case document: evidence mapped against Manager requirements, gaps closed during this path, your succession plan (proving you can be replaced), and the specific ask. Rehearse with AI or a mentor before the real conversation.

AI Application

Ask: "Role-play as my manager — the budget-constrained, already-has-two-candidates version. Challenge my promotion case. What's weak? What's missing? What would change your mind?"

Session Recap — Why it matters

The move to Manager is granted on evidence, timing, and trust — not on hope. Build the case deliberately.

I have a documented promotion case with evidence against Manager competencies
I understand the plateau risks and what I can control
I have a plan for both "yes" and "not yet" outcomes
