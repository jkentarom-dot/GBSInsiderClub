DO THE WORK WELL

Run your daily work to standard and improve it — this is what you are paid for.

Why this matters

Delivering your daily work to a steady standard is what you are paid for first. Many people stay busy for years without improving how the work is done. This pillar shows you how to run the work well, and make it better over time.

What you’ll be able to do
  • Apply Lean (removing waste) and Six Sigma (reducing errors) basics to remove waste from a real process.
  • Map a process end to end and write a clear SOP (standard operating procedure) others can follow.
  • Run a small improvement that you can measure and show.
Who this is for

Anyone who delivers process work and wants to move from doing tasks to owning and improving them.

FROM THE FIELD

Have you ever been beaten up for your KPIs?

I remember sitting in a performance review early in my career. Most of our KPIs were red.

The first question was simple.

“What are the root causes?”

We explained them.

“ERP issues. Poor master data. Incomplete requests from upstream teams. Approvals stuck in other functions. Constant firefighting. Demand spikes. Too many manual workarounds.”

The list was long.

The response?

“Okay… now fix it.”

That was the frustrating part. Because there wasn’t one root cause. There were dozens — a web of small issues spread across different systems, teams, processes and functions, many of them completely outside our control.

Yet the KPI belonged to us. It felt unfair.

Years later, after leading international Shared Services teams, I realized something important.

KPIs don’t measure effort. They measure outcomes.

And outcomes are rarely created by one team alone. They’re shaped by process design, technology, data quality, capacity, upstream discipline, and cross-functional collaboration — the maturity of the entire operating model.

Great operations leaders understand this. They don’t treat a KPI as a verdict. They treat it as the starting point of a diagnosis.

What this means for you

If you’re building your career in GBS, don’t stop at reporting the number. Learn to explain the system behind it. Talk about what you’re doing to improve the broken parts underneath. Name the partners you work with in other teams. Share your roadmap and the wins along the way. No finger-pointing — solutions.

And, more importantly, learn to spot the weak signals before they become tomorrow’s KPI problem. It’s one of the highest-value habits you can build early.

Because the people who create the biggest impact aren’t the ones with perfect dashboards. They’re the ones who can untangle complex problems, bring the right people together, and improve the system — not just the score.

2.1 Process and Knowledge Management

Process Documentation Standards (SOPs and DTPs)
Pro
+

Two documentation types, often confused:

  • SOPs define how work is done.
  • DTPs define step-by-step execution.

Most GBS teams have documentation that is either too abstract to be useful or so detailed it is never read. The sweet spot lets someone new execute the process within a day — not a week, not a month.

Knowledge Management: Maintaining the Single Source of Truth
Pro
+

In a GBS environment, knowledge is distributed across wikis, SharePoint sites, email threads, and heads. When someone leaves, their knowledge walks out with them.

  • A single source of truth is not a technology problem — it is a discipline problem.
  • One authoritative location per process, updated as part of the change cycle, not as an afterthought.

End-to-End Process Ownership and Handoffs

The biggest source of errors in GBS is not what happens inside a team — it is what happens between teams. Handoff points are where transactions stall, data gets corrupted, and accountability disappears. E2E process ownership means someone is accountable for the outcome, not just their piece of the chain.

2.2 Service Management

ITIL/COBIT Fundamentals for Business Services
Team Lead
+

ITIL and COBIT were built for IT service management — GBS borrowed them because the underlying logic holds.

  • ITIL covers the full service lifecycle: incident management, problem management, change management, service catalog.
  • COBIT adds the governance and control layer on top.
  • Not every concept translates directly to GBS, but the service lifecycle thinking is universally applicable.

Service Catalog: Defining and Pricing the Menu
Pro
+

A service catalog lists what your GBS delivers, to whom, at what service level, and at what cost.

  • Without one, your GBS is a black box — business units do not know what they are paying for, and you cannot manage demand or demonstrate value.
  • Building a catalog forces uncomfortable conversations about what is in scope and what is not.

Managing Service Transitions and Operations
Project Mgr
+

Service transition is the handover from project to BAU — where a new process or team goes live. It is where most GBS implementations stumble. The project team moves on, operations inherits undocumented exceptions, and the first month looks nothing like the business case. Structured transition management prevents this.

Customer Experience Metrics: NPS and CES
Team Lead
+

Two experience metrics that measure different things:

  • NPS — whether internal clients would recommend your service.
  • CES — how hard it was to get what they needed.

In GBS, CES is often more actionable than NPS — a process can be accurate but so painful to interact with that stakeholders route around it entirely.

Experience Level Agreements (XLAs) vs. SLAs
Project Mgr
+

Same service, two very different questions:

  • SLA — what you deliver ("resolved within 24 hours").
  • XLA — how it felt: did the resolution help, was communication clear, did the stakeholder have to follow up.

XLAs shift the conversation from compliance to quality.

2.3 Continuous Improvement

CI Culture: Building the Kaizen Mindset
Team Lead
+

Continuous improvement only works when it is embedded in daily operations, not run as a quarterly initiative from a PMO slide deck. Kaizen means every team member looks for small improvements every day. The team lead’s job is creating the environment where people feel safe to flag problems and empowered to fix them.

The 8 Wastes (Muda): Recognizing Value Leakage
Rookie
+

Lean identifies eight types of waste: defects, overproduction, waiting, non-utilized talent, transportation, inventory, motion, and extra-processing.

  • Waiting — approvals stuck in queues
  • Defects — rework from incorrect data
  • Non-utilized talent — experienced analysts doing manual copy-paste work

Team Lead
+

Visual management makes work status visible to everyone without asking. A huddle board shows daily priorities, blockers, and KPIs at a glance. Digital Kanban tools do the same for distributed teams. The point is not the board — it is the daily standup conversation that happens in front of it.

Lean Six Sigma Fundamentals (The Belt System)
Pro
+

The Lean Six Sigma belt system, in plain terms:

  • White — awareness.
  • Yellow — participation.
  • Green — part-time project leadership.
  • Black — full-time improvement leadership.
  • Master Black Belt — organizational capability building.

The belt itself matters less than the structured problem-solving methodology it represents.

SIPOC Diagrams: Defining Scope and Boundaries
Pro
+

SIPOC stands for Suppliers, Inputs, Process, Outputs, Customers. It is a one-page scope definition that prevents improvement projects from drifting. Before you optimize anything, you need to agree on where the process starts, where it ends, and who it serves. SIPOC forces that alignment in a single workshop.

Value Stream Mapping: Current vs. Future State
Project Mgr
+

A value stream map traces a transaction from request to delivery, capturing every step, wait time, and rework loop. The current-state map usually reveals that 80% of total cycle time is waiting, not processing. The future-state map is your improvement roadmap — the gap between the two is your business case.

DMAIC Framework: Define Measure Analyze Improve Control
Project Mgr
+

DMAIC is the Six Sigma project methodology:

  • Define the problem.
  • Measure current state.
  • Analyze root causes.
  • Improve with tested solutions.
  • Control to sustain gains.

Every step has specific deliverables and tollgates. Skipping straight to Improve — which everyone wants to do — is how you solve the wrong problem.

A3 Problem Solving: Structured Logic and Storytelling
Team Lead
+

The A3 format constrains your entire problem-solving narrative to one sheet of paper:

  • Left side — background, current condition, root cause.
  • Right side — target condition, countermeasures, implementation plan.

The constraint forces clarity. If you cannot explain the problem and solution on one page, you do not understand it well enough.

Root Cause Analysis: 5 Whys and Fishbone Diagrams
Team Lead
+

Two root-cause tools that stop you fixing symptoms:

  • Five Whys — keep asking "why" until you reach the systemic cause.
  • Fishbone — categorize potential causes by type.

When the same error type recurs quarterly, someone stopped at "why" number two.

Innovation Management: Hackathons and Idea Funnels
Project Mgr
+

Innovation and continuous improvement are different disciplines — CI optimizes what exists; innovation creates something new.

  • Hackathons generate energy and ideas, but without an idea funnel to evaluate, fund, and implement, they become annual morale events with no lasting output.
  • The funnel matters more than the hackathon.

Validating Savings: Hard (P&L) vs. Soft (Avoidance)
Project Mgr
+

Not all savings are equal — and Finance knows the difference.

  • Hard savings hit the P&L: headcount reduction, vendor cost elimination, actual budget decrease.
  • Soft savings are cost avoidance — you would have needed 5 more people if you had not automated.
  • Finance only trusts hard savings. Your GBS credibility depends on knowing which category your improvement lands in — and being honest about it.

2.4 Operational Controls

Quality Management: Error Tracking and Prevention
Team Lead
+

The difference is timing:

  • Quality control — tracking errors after they happen.
  • Quality management — preventing errors before they happen.

The shift from reactive to proactive requires categorizing defects by root cause, building prevention controls, and measuring defect rates over time — not just counting how many got caught in review.

Read full guide →
The Four-Eyes Principle and Internal Controls
Rookie
+

The four-eyes principle means no single person can initiate and complete a transaction alone.

  • In GBS, this is the foundation of internal controls: segregation of duties between who creates, who reviews, and who approves.
  • It exists because the cost of catching fraud or errors after they reach the client is exponentially higher than catching them at execution.

Audit Preparation: Being Audit Ready Every Day
Pro
+

Audit readiness is not a quarterly fire drill — it is a daily operating discipline.

  • Documentation is current, controls are evidenced in real-time, exceptions are logged with explanations.
  • Your team can walk an auditor through any transaction without advance preparation.
  • The teams that panic before audits are the ones that treat compliance as separate from operations.

Handling Escalations, Volume Spikes and SLA Breaches
Pro
+

When volumes spike or SLAs breach, the first response matters more than the root cause analysis that follows. Effective escalation management means pre-defined severity levels, automatic stakeholder notification, and a triage protocol that prioritizes by business impact — not by who escalates loudest.

2.5 Personal Productivity

Workflow Prioritization (Eisenhower Matrix)
Pro
+

The Eisenhower Matrix sorts tasks by urgency and importance. In GBS, the trap is spending 80% of your day in "urgent and important" (fire-fighting) while "important but not urgent" (process improvement, career development) never gets touched. The matrix is simple. The discipline to protect quadrant two time is not.

Email Management and Zero Inbox Methodologies
Rookie
+

In a GBS environment, email is both your primary communication tool and your biggest productivity drain. Zero inbox means processing every email into an action, a delegate, a file, or a delete within a defined time window. The goal is decision speed, not email bankruptcy.

Time Blocking and Deep Work in a Service Environment
Rookie
+

Service environments interrupt constantly — queries, escalations, status updates. Time blocking reserves specific hours for focused work and communicates those blocks to the team. Without it, your day is 100% reactive and deliverables that need thinking never get done.
