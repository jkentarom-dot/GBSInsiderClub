2.1 Process and Knowledge Management

Process Documentation Standards (SOPs and DTPs)
Pro
+

SOPs define how work is done. DTPs define step-by-step execution. Most GBS teams have documentation that is either too abstract to be useful or so detailed it is never read. The sweet spot is documentation that lets someone new execute the process within a day — not a week, not a month.

Read full guide →
Knowledge Management: Maintaining the Single Source of Truth
Pro
+

In a GBS environment, knowledge is distributed across wikis, SharePoint sites, email threads, and heads. When someone leaves, their knowledge walks out with them. A single source of truth is not a technology problem — it is a discipline problem. One authoritative location per process, updated as part of the change cycle, not as an afterthought.

End-to-End Process Ownership and Handoffs
Project Mgr
+

The biggest source of errors in GBS is not what happens inside a team — it is what happens between teams. Handoff points are where transactions stall, data gets corrupted, and accountability disappears. E2E process ownership means someone is accountable for the outcome, not just their piece of the chain.

Tools for Process Mapping (Visio, Lucid, Signavio)
Pro
+

Process maps are the common language of GBS improvement. Visio is the legacy standard, Lucidchart is the collaboration-first alternative, and Signavio connects process mining to documentation. The tool matters less than the discipline: if your process maps do not match reality, they are fiction.

Service Blueprinting and Design Thinking
Project Mgr
+

Service blueprinting maps the entire delivery chain — what the customer sees (front-stage) and what happens behind the scenes (back-stage). Design thinking applies human-centered problem solving to service improvement. Both are underused in GBS because most teams optimize for efficiency first and experience never.

2.2 Service Management

ITIL/COBIT Fundamentals for Business Services
Team Lead
+

ITIL provides a framework for managing services end-to-end — incident management, problem management, change management, service catalog. COBIT adds the governance and control layer. GBS borrowed these from IT service management, and while not every concept translates directly, the service lifecycle thinking is universally applicable.

Read full guide →
Service Catalog: Defining and Pricing the Menu
Pro
+

A service catalog lists what your GBS delivers, to whom, at what service level, and at what cost. Without one, your GBS is a black box — business units do not know what they are paying for, and you cannot manage demand or demonstrate value. Building a catalog forces uncomfortable conversations about what is in scope and what is not.

Read full guide →
Managing Service Transitions and Operations
Project Mgr
+

Service transition is the handover from project to BAU — where a new process or team goes live. It is where most GBS implementations stumble. The project team moves on, operations inherits undocumented exceptions, and the first month looks nothing like the business case. Structured transition management prevents this.

Read full guide →
Customer Experience Metrics: NPS and CES
Team Lead
+

Net Promoter Score measures whether internal clients would recommend your service. Customer Effort Score measures how hard it was to get what they needed. In GBS, CES is often more actionable than NPS — a process can be accurate but so painful to interact with that stakeholders route around it entirely.

Read full guide →
Experience Level Agreements (XLAs) vs. SLAs
Project Mgr
+

SLAs measure what you deliver. XLAs measure how it felt. An SLA might say "resolved within 24 hours." The XLA asks whether the resolution actually helped, whether communication was clear, and whether the stakeholder had to follow up. XLAs shift the conversation from compliance to quality.

2.3 Continuous Improvement

CI Culture: Building the Kaizen Mindset
Team Lead
+

Continuous improvement only works when it is embedded in daily operations, not run as a quarterly initiative from a PMO slide deck. Kaizen means every team member looks for small improvements every day. The team lead's job is creating the environment where people feel safe to flag problems and empowered to fix them.

Read full guide →
The 8 Wastes (Muda): Recognizing Value Leakage
Rookie
+

Lean identifies eight types of waste: defects, overproduction, waiting, non-utilized talent, transportation, inventory, motion, and extra-processing. In a GBS context, the biggest offenders are typically waiting (approvals stuck in queues), defects (rework from incorrect data), and non-utilized talent (experienced analysts doing manual copy-paste work).

Visual Management: Huddle Boards and Digital Kanban
Team Lead
+

Visual management makes work status visible to everyone without asking. A huddle board shows daily priorities, blockers, and KPIs at a glance. Digital Kanban tools do the same for distributed teams. The point is not the board — it is the daily standup conversation that happens in front of it.

Lean Six Sigma Fundamentals (The Belt System)
Pro
+

White Belt is awareness. Yellow is participation. Green is project leadership part-time. Black is full-time improvement leadership. Master Black Belt is organizational capability building. The belt itself is less important than the structured problem-solving methodology it represents.

SIPOC Diagrams: Defining Scope and Boundaries
Pro
+

SIPOC stands for Suppliers, Inputs, Process, Outputs, Customers. It is a one-page scope definition that prevents improvement projects from drifting. Before you optimize anything, you need to agree on where the process starts, where it ends, and who it serves. SIPOC forces that alignment in a single workshop.

Value Stream Mapping: Current vs. Future State
Project Mgr
+

A value stream map traces a transaction from request to delivery, capturing every step, wait time, and rework loop. The current-state map usually reveals that 80% of total cycle time is waiting, not processing. The future-state map is your improvement roadmap — the gap between the two is your business case.

DMAIC Framework: Define Measure Analyze Improve Control
Project Mgr
+

DMAIC is the Six Sigma project methodology. Define the problem. Measure current state. Analyze root causes. Improve with tested solutions. Control to sustain gains. Every step has specific deliverables and tollgates. Skipping straight to Improve — which everyone wants to do — is how you solve the wrong problem.

Read full guide →
A3 Problem Solving: Structured Logic and Storytelling
Team Lead
+

The A3 format constrains your entire problem-solving narrative to one sheet of paper. Left side: background, current condition, root cause. Right side: target condition, countermeasures, implementation plan. The constraint forces clarity. If you cannot explain the problem and solution on one page, you do not understand it well enough.

Root Cause Analysis: 5 Whys and Fishbone Diagrams
Team Lead
+

Five Whys is exactly what it sounds like — keep asking "why" until you reach the systemic cause. Fishbone diagrams categorize potential causes by type. Both prevent the GBS trap of fixing symptoms instead of causes. When the same error type recurs quarterly, someone stopped at "why" number two.

Innovation Management: Hackathons and Idea Funnels
Project Mgr
+

Innovation and continuous improvement are different disciplines. CI optimizes what exists. Innovation creates something new. Hackathons generate energy and ideas but without an idea funnel to evaluate, fund, and implement, they become annual morale events with no lasting output. The funnel matters more than the hackathon.

Read full guide →
Validating Savings: Hard (P&L) vs. Soft (Avoidance)
Project Mgr
+

Hard savings hit the P&L — headcount reduction, vendor cost elimination, actual budget decrease. Soft savings are cost avoidance — you would have needed 5 more people if you had not automated. Finance only trusts hard savings. Your GBS credibility depends on knowing the difference and being honest about which category your improvement lands in.

2.4 Operational Controls

Quality Management: Error Tracking and Prevention
Team Lead
+

Tracking errors after they happen is quality control. Preventing errors before they happen is quality management. The shift from reactive to proactive requires categorizing defects by root cause, building prevention controls, and measuring defect rates over time — not just counting how many got caught in review.

Read full guide →
The Four-Eyes Principle and Internal Controls
Rookie
+

The four-eyes principle means no single person can initiate and complete a transaction alone. In GBS, this is the foundation of internal controls — segregation of duties between who creates, who reviews, and who approves. It exists because the cost of catching fraud or errors after they reach the client is exponentially higher than catching them at execution.

Read full guide →
Audit Preparation: Being Audit Ready Every Day
Pro
+

Audit readiness is not a quarterly fire drill — it is a daily operating discipline. Documentation is current, controls are evidenced in real-time, exceptions are logged with explanations, and your team can walk an auditor through any transaction without advance preparation. The teams that panic before audits are the ones that treat compliance as separate from operations.

Read full guide →
Handling Escalations, Volume Spikes and SLA Breaches
Pro
+

When volumes spike or SLAs breach, the first response matters more than the root cause analysis that follows. Effective escalation management means pre-defined severity levels, automatic stakeholder notification, and a triage protocol that prioritizes by business impact — not by who escalates loudest.

2.5 Personal Productivity

Workflow Prioritization (Eisenhower Matrix)
Pro
+

The Eisenhower Matrix sorts tasks by urgency and importance. In GBS, the trap is spending 80% of your day in "urgent and important" (fire-fighting) while "important but not urgent" (process improvement, career development) never gets touched. The matrix is simple. The discipline to protect quadrant two time is not.

Read full guide →
Email Management and Zero Inbox Methodologies
Rookie
+

In a GBS environment, email is both your primary communication tool and your biggest productivity drain. Zero inbox is not about having zero emails — it is about processing every email into an action, a delegate, a file, or a delete within a defined time window. The goal is decision speed, not email bankruptcy.

Time Blocking and Deep Work in a Service Environment
Rookie
+

Service environments interrupt constantly — queries, escalations, status updates. Time blocking reserves specific hours for focused work and communicates those blocks to the team. Without it, your day is 100% reactive and deliverables that need thinking never get done.
